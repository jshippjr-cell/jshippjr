## 3. Connection doctrine

producers; business affairs, legal, procurement. Editors and supervisors as referral seats.
Creative directors: follow and comment, never request.

**How many.** Fifteen a week (§10): eight Tuesday, seven Thursday. Weeks 1–2 the thirty,

credit as evidence before the request goes.

**The sentence** (under 300 characters; their credit, their thing):

- *Producer / HoP:* "Your producer credit on [title] came up on [source]. I write on Mondays about how agencies buy music and would rather learn it from someone who does. No pitch."
- *Executive producer:* "[Company]'s credit on [title]. I run a small music house in the Southeast and follow who is producing here. Nothing to sell you; I'd like to read what you ship."
- *Business affairs / legal:* "Your [byline / panel] on [topic]. I sign a rights document on every job and would value knowing how counsel reads one. Not a request for advice."

- *Supervisor:* "Your credit on [title]. When a job of mine is catalogue-shaped I'd rather know where to send it than pretend it isn't."

**Never in a request:** a link; a pitch; availability, rates, "if you ever need"; a reel;

compliment without a credit. No second request: thirty days' silence is a no; a no holds
six months. Typed by hand; anything sendable to two people is wrong.

### Wk 1 · Mon 14 Sep · How Agencies Actually Buy Music 1

**The music number is not the campaign number.**

When a producer tells me the budget, I ask a second question: what is the music number?
Not production, not media — the line that pays for the music, and whether it includes the
licence.

Three answers come back. A music line with the licence inside it. A music line with the
licence as somebody else's problem, usually business affairs, usually later. No music line
yet; music comes out of whatever is left.

Each is fine. Each prices differently, because the creative fee is what it costs to write
and record the cue and the licence fee is what the *use* is worth — media, territory, term,
exclusivity. Fold them into one number and nobody can see which moved when the media plan
changes.

So ask for the music number by name, and whether it carries the licence.

### Wk 2 · Mon 21 Sep · Music Myths 1

**~~Royalty-free means free of copyright.~~**

Royalty-free is a licence term. The licensor is not collecting a royalty per use; the
copyright stays with whoever owns it, and the licence still has edges — media, term,
territory. Where a library has registered its catalogue for content matching, a claim can
still land on the client's channel with a valid licence in hand **(verify platform terms
before publication)**.

The question before signing: what does this cover, and who do I call when a claim arrives?
Sources on the image. Not legal advice.

### Wk 2 · Fri 25 Sep · Producer's Notebook 2

**What I got wrong about pricing.**

The first version of our quote did something I would have called reasonable a year ago: it
read the client's budget and landed on it. The brief said sixty; the quote said sixty. It
looked like listening. It was an echo.

An echo teaches nobody anything. If the number is always the budget, the client never
learns what the licence is worth, the studio never learns what the work costs, and the
first time a media plan grows after signature nobody can say which part of the number
should move.

So the model now prices two fees and does not look at the budget until both are done. The
creative fee is what it costs to make the work well — writer, players, room, mix — at a
stated margin. The licence fee is what the use is worth: media, territory, term,
exclusivity, multiplied and capped, because four factors compound faster than the market.
Then the budget comes in — not to set the price, but to return a verdict: below the floor,
in the band, above it. Below the floor we offer the reduced scope that fits, or say *not
this one*, in writing.

I will not print a number here. The proposal is the only place a client sees one; a figure
on a post is a rate card by another name. What I will say is that the factors are priors,
ratified against published research and not yet tested on a paid job, and that any licence
input a brief does not state is named as assumed beside the number rather than quietly
filled in.

I got it wrong by trying to be agreeable. The fix was to be legible.

### Wk 3 · Mon 28 Sep · How Agencies Actually Buy Music 2

**Does the term run from delivery or from first air?**

A twelve-month term is not twelve months of use. It is twelve months from a date, and the
date is the part nobody writes down.

From delivery: the clock starts the day the package lands. A spot that waits eleven weeks
for a media window has spent a fifth of its term in a folder.

From first air: the clock starts when the campaign does. The client gets the year they
pictured; the studio carries an open date and prices for it.

Neither is wrong. They price differently, and the difference is visible only if the brief
says which it means. It is one line. Write it in.

### Wk 3 · Fri 2 Oct · Producer's Notebook 3

**Why the second button says "Ask for a new version".**

For a while the room's second button said *Request changes*. It is what every review
surface says, and I copied it without thinking.

Then I walked a reviewer through the AURORA demonstration and watched them read *Request
changes* as the place you leave a note. Of course they did. In their world a change is a
sentence — *the strings come in late* — not a decision to spend a round. The button was
asking a small question in the words of a large one.

So it now says *Ask for a new version*, and beside it, before you press, the room says what
that spends: one round, one new version. While a version is owed the button is not offered
at all; the room says *we're already writing your next version* instead. Notes stay free
and stay where they are. The decision is the only thing that costs, and it is named as a
decision.

The same day, the Approve button got its own list — locks the master, spends no round,
releases no payment, can be undone. Two buttons, each honest about what it commits.

What this taught me is that most of a process is vocabulary. A client cannot see a rule;
they can see a word on a button, and the word is the rule as far as they are concerned.
Borrow the word from somewhere else and you borrow the rule.

I will keep changing words when someone reads one wrong, and say so here when I do.

### Wk 4 · Mon 5 Oct · Music Myths 2

**~~The platform's music library covers our paid posts.~~**

The commercial music libraries inside the social platforms are licensed for posts on that
platform, and the terms distinguish organic use from paid promotion and from reuse
elsewhere **(verify each platform's current terms before publication)**. A boosted post, a
cross-posted cut or a cutdown on another channel can sit outside the licence; the usual
first sign is muted audio or a claim on the client's page.

The question: where will this run, and does the licence say so. Sources on the image. Not
legal advice.

## The measure

> **If Chordential stopped posting tomorrow, would the relationships we've built
> continue creating opportunities?**

Everything below is built to make the answer *yes*. A relationship counts only if it
would survive our silence: a person who would answer the phone, make an introduction,
or send a brief without having seen anything from us in ninety days.

Two consequences of the inheritance shape every stage:

1. **Lean means people, not spend.** No sponsorships, no booths, no paid memberships
   until a relationship has already made the room worth being in.
2. **Honesty applies to relationships.** We never imply clients we don't have, never
   name-drop a person as a reference without their say-so, and never present a
   conversation as an endorsement.

## Stage 1 — The commercial music ecosystem

### How a piece of advertising music actually gets commissioned

The chain, in the order money and influence move through it:

1. **The brand** approves a campaign and a budget (brand manager / marketing
   director; procurement sets vendor terms at larger brands).
2. **The agency** wins or holds the account (account director owns the budget
   conversation; agency founder or CEO at small and mid-size shops).
3. **The creative team** develops the spot (creative director, ACD, copywriter, art
   director). Their taste sets the music direction; they rarely pick the vendor.
4. **The agency producer** (integrated / broadcast / content producer) turns the idea
   into a production: writes the music brief, decides who is invited to demo, runs
   the bid, manages the vendor. **This is the seat that decides who gets the call.**
5. **The executive producer / head of production** oversees producers, keeps the
   preferred-vendor roster, approves budgets, and carries vendor relationships
   between jobs.
6. **The music supervisor** (agency-side music department at the large networks;
   independent at most others) sources, negotiates and clears music when the budget
   has one. When present, they are the gatekeeper.
7. **The production company and director** shoot the spot; the director shapes tone
   and often the demo track.
8. **The editor / edit house** cuts the spot to a temp track. Temp love is the single
   most common way a music decision is made before anyone is hired.
9. **Audio post** (sound design, mix) finishes the sound; sometimes supplies music;
   often recommends the music house.
10. **Business affairs / legal** at the agency clears the rights and signs the
    licence. They can veto anything with murky paperwork. **This is the seat our
    positioning speaks to directly.**
11. **In-house brand studios / marketing operations** now commission social and
    branded content directly, without an agency, on short turnaround.
12. **Peer music houses and composers** overflow work to each other and refer when a
    brief is off-craft for them.

### Rank by influence over "who gets hired to make original music"

1. **Agency producer / integrated producer.** Writes the brief, picks who demos,
   moves between agencies and carries their vendor list with them.
2. **Executive producer / head of production.** Owns the roster and the budget.
   One EP is fifteen producers' default.
3. **Music supervisor.** Where one exists, nobody gets hired around them.
4. **Business affairs / legal.** The clearance gate. Our product exists to make
   their job boring; they are the natural advocate for "use the people whose
   paperwork is clean."
5. **Editor / edit house.** The temp track is the music decision more often than
   anyone admits, and editors are asked "who should we call?" constantly.
6. **Creative director.** Sets taste and can kill a track; rarely sources one.
7. **In-house content lead / marketing ops.** The fastest-growing direct buyer, on
   the shortest turnarounds, usually with no music process at all.
8. **Brand manager / marketing director.** Signs, funds, and remembers who made
   the last campaign painless.
9. **Audio post / mixer.** Final mile; hears every music vendor's stems and knows
   who delivers clean files.
10. **Agency founder / principal.** Everything at once, at the shops that fit our
    price band.
11. **Director / production company EP.** Tone and demo influence; occasional
    source of direct work (director's cuts, spec, brand films).
12. **Procurement.** Matters at large brands and holding companies; a later-phase
    relationship, not a first one.
13. **Account director.** Budget conversation; rarely a music opinion.
14. **Peer composers / music houses.** No hiring power; real referral power.

**The strategic reading.** Seats 1, 2, 4 and 5 are where relationships convert to
briefs. Seat 4 is under-served by every music house (nobody courts business affairs)
and is the one seat that already cares about what we uniquely offer. Seats 6–8 are
where reputation is confirmed, not where it is created.

### Seat by seat: why, how it begins, how it develops, what we give first

**1. Agency producers (20).**
*Why:* they decide who demos. *Begins:* a specific, informed question about how they
run music on a job — never a pitch; a mutual editor or mixer; an ad-club event; a
shared panel audience. *Develops:* they send a brief they can't fill or a question
about a licence; we answer usefully whether or not there's work in it. *We give first:*
a plain-language reading of any music licence they're unsure of; what a scope should
cost (we have a priced model — `/pricing` — and can say so honestly); a composer
introduction for a job we're wrong for.

**2. Executive producers / heads of production (10).**
*Why:* roster and budget. *Begins:* almost always through one of their producers or an
editor; occasionally through a trade org where they sit on a committee. *Develops:*
after one job delivered without a single follow-up email, or after we've saved one of
their producers from a clearance problem. *We give first:* the delivery-package
standard itself — cue sheet, clearance certificate, versioned files — offered as a
checklist they can hold every vendor to, not just us.

**3. Creative directors / ACDs (10).**
*Why:* taste and veto. *Begins:* through the producer, or through shared enthusiasm
for a piece of work (theirs, never ours). *Develops:* slowly; CDs remember who
understood the idea, not who sold hardest. *We give first:* an honest, specific
reaction to work they made; references and listening they didn't know about.

**4. Music supervisors (8).**
*Why:* gatekeepers where they exist, and the most connected people in the chain.
*Begins:* Guild of Music Supervisors events, sync conferences, or a supervisor's
public request for something specific we can genuinely help with. *Develops:* by being
the studio that returns clean stems, accurate metadata and a cue sheet without being
asked. *We give first:* clearance and chain-of-title thinking on original work
(their pain is submissions and paperwork, not a shortage of music); composer
introductions.

**5. Editors / edit-house producers (10).**
*Why:* temp love and "who should we call". *Begins:* naturally — editors and composers
share a craft language; a conversation about a cut. *Develops:* by making their job
easier: fast cutdowns, versions that hit their frames, no surprises in the mix.
*We give first:* stems and alternates cut for the edit; a standing offer to solve
temp-track replacement problems.

**6. Audio post / mixers / sound designers (6).**
*Why:* they hear every vendor's files and are asked for referrals. *Begins:* on a job,
or through AES / MPSE circles. *Develops:* by delivering files the way a mixer wants
them. *We give first:* clean deliverables, credit where it's due, and referrals to
them when a client needs sound design we don't do.

**7. Business affairs / clearance / entertainment counsel (6).**
*Why:* the clearance gate, and the seat nobody courts. *Begins:* with a question they
find interesting — the AI copyrightability rulings, Content ID on paid social, MFN
splits on original work. *Develops:* when they start sending colleagues to ask us
things. *We give first:* our clearance certificate and licence language, offered for
their critique; a careful, cited read of the rulings that affect them.

**8. Brand-side marketing and in-house content leads (8).**
*Why:* the direct buyer on the shortest turnaround. *Begins:* ANA and local marketing
associations, or an introduction from an agency producer who used to work in-house.
*Develops:* by teaching them the music process they don't have — what to ask for, what
a licence should cover, what things cost. *We give first:* exactly that, as advice.

**9. Agency founders / principals (5).**
*Why:* at a 10–60 person shop, one person is the roster, the budget and the signature.
*Begins:* founder-to-founder, usually through ad-club or chamber networks; they are
easier to reach than their producers, not harder. *Develops:* by being a peer with a
useful opinion about running a small creative business. *We give first:* frank
conversation about pricing, vendors and process — we are building the operating
system for exactly their problem.

**10. Production company EPs / reps (5).**
*Why:* they see every agency's production calendar. *Begins:* AICP chapter events,
film-commission networks, a shared shoot. *Develops:* by being the music house they
can put in front of a director without embarrassment. *We give first:* fast demo
music for pitches and treatments, when we choose to; referrals for crew.

**11. Peer composers / music house principals (5).**
*Why:* overflow and referrals run both ways, and they are the only people who fully
understand the job. *Begins:* SCL, AMP, composer communities; honest admiration of
their work. *Develops:* by sending them work we're wrong for, first. *We give first:*
referrals, and the delivery tooling — the packaging discipline is a thing peers will
want to borrow.

**12. Connectors (4).**
*Why:* trade editors, podcast hosts and chapter leads meet everyone and remember who
was useful. *Begins:* by being a source — someone with a real, cited point of view on
rights, AI and pricing. *Develops:* when they call us to check a fact. *We give first:*
accurate information, fast, with no ask attached.

**13. Educators (3).**
*Why:* the producers and creatives of five years from now, and a credible stage.
*Begins:* offering a guest session on how music is actually commissioned and cleared.
*Develops:* into a recurring slot and a pipeline of introductions in both directions.
*We give first:* the class, and honest career conversations with students.

### The first thirty

The sales playbook's "named list of 30" is the first tranche of these hundred,
weighted toward seats 1, 5, 7 and 9 in the Southeast — the seats where a conversation
can begin without any reputation because we are asking, not offering. A seat is
"filled" only when the person exists in `buyer_person` with evidence behind them and
has moved past Cold on their own initiative (see Stage 8).

## Stage 4 — Networking framework

A repeatable rhythm, sized to the playbook's *ten real conversations a week* and to a
founder who also runs the studio.

### Cadence

| Rhythm | What happens | Count |
|---|---|---|
| **Daily** (20 min) | Read what the hundred are doing — new work, new jobs, awards, moves. Log evidence, not impressions. React to one thing genuinely, in public or private. | 1 touch |
| **Weekly** | Ten real conversations: calls, coffees, DMs that go somewhere. At least three with people we have *no* commercial reason to talk to yet. One introduction made between two people who should know each other. | 10 conversations, 1 intro |
| **Monthly** | One room (Tier A event). Two "how are you, no reason" reconnects with Warm people. Review the hundred: who moved, who went quiet, who we owe. | 1 room, 2 reconnects |
| **Quarterly** | One deliberate act of generosity with no path to revenue (a clearance workshop for a post house's staff; a pricing session for a founder; a class). Re-rank the seats. | 1 act |
| **Yearly** | One conference where three known people will be. Write down, for each of the hundred, what we did for them this year. If the answer is nothing, they are not a relationship. | |

### How to introduce yourself

Not scripts — rules.

1. **Lead with their world.** The opening is a specific, informed question about how
   *they* handle music (sourcing, clearance, cost, files), earned by having looked at
   their work. Curiosity is the only credible reason a stranger has for talking.
2. **Say what we are in one honest sentence** — original music, delivered with the
   rights paperwork a procurement team wants — and then stop. The positioning is
   already written; don't improvise a second one.
3. **Never mention availability, rates, or "if you ever need."** Those three phrases
   turn a conversation into a solicitation.
4. **Be specific about what we can't do.** "We don't do library, we don't do AI, we
   don't do sound design in-house" is more memorable than any capability list, and it
   is the honesty rule doing its job.
5. **Ask for advice before anything else.** People remember whom they helped far more
   than who helped them.

### How to follow up

- **Within 24 hours:** one note that references a specific thing they said. No
  attachment, no deck, no link to us unless they asked.
- **Deliver anything promised before you do anything else** — an introduction, a
  reading of a licence, a name. Speed of follow-through is the first evidence of
  what working with us will be like.
- **Then wait.** The next touch is prompted by *their* event (new work shipped, a
  move, a post, an award) or by something genuinely useful to them, not by our
  calendar. A follow-up with no reason is a nudge; a nudge is a small withdrawal.
- **Log it** (Stage 8). The follow-up is only as good as the memory behind the next
  one.

### How to remain memorable

- **Own one topic.** We are the people who know what a licence should say, what a
  cue sheet is for, and what music should cost. Every producer knows twenty
  composers; almost none know one who can explain paid-social Content ID exposure.
- **Be the same person in every room.** Memorability is consistency, not flair.
- **Deliver small things impeccably.** A two-line answer, sent fast and right, is
  remembered longer than a long one sent late.
- **Make introductions that stick.** People remember the person who introduced them
  to their best editor for years.
- **Show the work, not the pitch.** When we do have work, the delivery package
  (cue sheet, certificate, organised files, review room) is the artefact people
  forward to each other. It sells without a word from us.

### Building without asking for work

The founding rule: **we never ask for a brief.** We ask for opinions, introductions,
critique of our paperwork, and time. Briefs arrive when someone has a problem and we
are the first name their memory returns. The playbook's three closes (a scoped call,
a written offer, a deposit) happen only *after* a buyer has raised a real job — the
relationship work ends where the sales conversation begins, and it never
manufactures the beginning.

Practically:

- Two-thirds of conversations should be with people who cannot give us work this
  year (editors, counsel, peers, educators, connectors).
- Every Warm relationship should have received at least one useful thing from us
  before we have received anything from them.
- When someone offers work we are wrong for, refer it to a peer, warmly and fast.
  That referral is worth more than the job.

### Becoming someone people enjoy hearing from

- **Be useful or be quiet.** A message that gives nothing is a cost to the reader.
- **Bring news they can use** — a ruling, a rate change, a person moving — with the
  source, not a hot take (evidence first, per the Constitution).
- **Celebrate their work publicly and specifically**, never our own by proxy.
- **Never turn a personal message into a pitch**, ever. One violation undoes a year.
- **Be brief.** Respect for time is the most under-used form of charm in this
  industry.

## Stage 5 — Thought leadership

Content and editorial belong to the CMO; this stage governs *behaviour* — how the
founder participates, disagrees and earns standing — and sets the long-term authority
thesis that the content strategy can serve.

### How respected founders in this industry behave

- They talk about **the craft and the business, not themselves.** The respected
  music-house founders are known for opinions about how spots get made, how music
  gets paid for, and what is going wrong in the trade — not for their reel.
- They **cite.** A claim about sync fees, AI copyright or Content ID comes with the
  ruling, the report, the number. That is exactly the Constitution's "evidence
  first" rule, in public.
- They **credit generously**: composers, editors, producers by name (with consent),
  every time.
- They **say what they don't know** and what they got wrong, in public, promptly.
- They **show up in the same rooms for years**. Standing is mostly attendance with
  a point of view.
- They **never punch down** at clients, juniors, or competitors, and they never
  post about a client's job without the client.

### What they publish (the remit we hand the CMO)

Not a content plan — the *authority thesis* the content strategy should serve:

> **Chordential is the voice for the rights, cost and delivery side of commercial
> music** — what a licence must cover, why AI-only output can't be cleanly licensed
> or insured, what original music should cost and why, how a cue sheet turns into a
> royalty, and what a procurement-grade music delivery looks like.

This is the ground nobody else wants and everybody needs: composers find it dull,
libraries find it inconvenient, and agencies find it terrifying. It is the one
subject on which a company with no reputation can be credible on day one, because it
is provable and cited rather than a matter of taste. The `/pricing` page, the
clearance certificate, the cue sheet and the delivery package are the artefacts; the
market-research and pricing-research documents are the evidence base.

### How they participate in discussions

- **Answer the question actually asked**, with the specific fact, and stop.
- **Add the missing piece**, not the opposing piece. "Also worth knowing:" beats
  "Actually."
- **Refer to better sources than yourself** when they exist. Pointing at the
  Copyright Office's own guidance is more authoritative than paraphrasing it.
- **Stay in lane.** A view on licensing is expected; a view on a CD's creative is not.
- **Never use a thread to reach a person.** If a conversation should be private, take
  it private and say nothing about it in public.

### How they disagree

1. **Only on matters of fact or consequence**, never taste.
2. **Steel-man first.** State the other view better than its author did.
3. **Bring the source.** A disagreement without a citation is an opinion, and
   opinions are not why anyone follows a music house.
4. **Concede the parts that are right**, specifically.
5. **Stop after one exchange.** The audience decides; the third reply is always a loss.
6. **Never in a client's or a prospect's thread.** Disagree with the industry, not
   with the buyer.

### The long-term authority strategy

| Horizon | Objective | Evidence it worked |
|---|---|---|
| **Year 1** | Be *the* answer in Southeast rooms to "who understands music rights?" One topic, cited, consistent. Guest lectures and local panels. | Producers and counsel send colleagues to ask us questions. |
| **Year 2** | Be a source for the trade press and podcast hosts on rights, AI and pricing. Sit on one association committee. The delivery standard is something peers borrow. | Trade press calls us to check a fact. A peer music house adopts our cue-sheet or certificate practice. |
| **Year 3** | Be a national voice on the business of commercial music — a recurring panel seat, a recognised standard (the clearance certificate) that agencies ask other vendors for. | An agency's business-affairs team asks another vendor for "the kind of paperwork Chordential provides." |
| **Year 5** | Be the operating layer's public face for how creative-service businesses run (Phase B/C, per the Constitution). | Other creative shops cite our practices when describing their own. |

The founder's public standing is the *only* marketing asset that survives a change
in posting cadence. That is why this document cares about it.

| **Creative agencies (10–60 people)** | A white-label music department with procurement-grade paperwork; they keep the client. We get repeat briefs. | Founder to founder; AAF chapters. | Make their producer look good. Deliver what we promised in the room. | Retainers (the playbook's Growth gate); the Phase B customer. |
| **Music supervisors (independent)** | We are a clean source of original work with rights sorted; they gain a vendor who never creates clearance work. | GMS; a supervisor's public ask. | Metadata, cue sheets and stems without being asked. | Being on their short list for original commissions. |

**Ordering under the lean constraint.** Edit houses, counsel and peer composers first

can reach a founder. Trade press and associations third, because standing there is
earned by the first two.

**Two partnerships we decline.** Any arrangement that pays for referrals (it poisons
the referral, and the honesty rule forbids presenting a paid introduction as a
recommendation), and any "preferred vendor" listing that requires us to claim
capabilities or clients we don't have.

## Stage 8 — The relationship dashboard (a CRM philosophy)

Behaviour, not software — but ChordOS already holds the memory (`buyer_person`,
`buyer_org`, `buyer_intel`), so the behaviour is written to fit the machine rather
than fight it. The stages are the product's own: **Cold → Warm → Engaged → Client**,
with **dormant** as a flag beside the stage, never a fifth stage (ADR-0057). The
product derives the stage; the human decides what to do about it.

### What we remember about people

Evidence or nothing — the ADR-0050 rule applied to memory. Every entry has a date and
a source; an empty field says "not yet known," never a guess.

| Remember | Why |
|---|---|
| **How they run music** on a job — who sources, who clears, what their last three music decisions were | Lets the next conversation start where the last one ended. |
| **What they care about** professionally — the work they're proud of, the problems they complain about | The only reliable basis for being useful. |
| **What we did for them, and when** | The ledger of goodwill. If it's empty after a year, the relationship isn't one. |
| **What they did for us** | So we never forget to return it. |
| **Who introduced whom** | Introductions are debts and credits in a network; the graph should show them. |
| **Their moves** — new agency, new role, new city | The single most reliable trigger for a genuine reconnect. |
| **Their people** — their editor, their mixer, their go-to composer | The adjacent seats, and a signal of where we'd fit. |
| **Personal, when offered** — kids, a marathon, a band | Only what they volunteered. Never researched. |
| **Their preference on contact** — channel, cadence, the "please don't" | Respect is remembered longer than help. |

What we do **not** record: anything scraped from private sources, anything about a
person's employer's confidential work, or any judgement of a person ("difficult",
"cheap"). The graph is a memory of relationships, not a dossier.

### How often we reconnect

| Stage | Trigger | Floor cadence | The rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cold** (known to us, no exchange) | Their event only — new work, a move, a public question | None | We don't reach out to Cold people on a schedule. Ever. |
| **Warm** (a real exchange, no work) | Their event, or something genuinely useful to them | 1 touch / quarter | Every touch gives something. Two touches without a reply and we stop until their next event. |
| **Engaged** (a live brief or active conversation) | The work | As the work needs | Over-communicate on the job; never on anything else. |
| **Client** (has paid us) | Delivery milestones; then their events; then a check-in with no ask | 1 touch / month for 3 months post-delivery, then 1 / quarter | Ask how the work performed. Ask what we could have done better. Never ask for the next job. |
| **Dormant flag** (Client or Engaged gone quiet — the product's 90-day rule) | The flag itself | One reconnect, no ask | *We have been paid by these people and have not spoken in months* is the most actionable pair in the layer. Reconnect with something useful; then follow their lead. |

The product proposes the reconnect (the next-best-action surface on `/buyers` and
`/relationships`); the founder decides whether today is the day and what to bring.
No touch is ever sent by the machine.

### When we help

- **Always, when asked**, at a cost we can afford — a reading of a licence, a name, an
  hour. The ledger fills from these.
- **Unasked, when we see their problem coming** — a ruling that affects their
  campaigns, a clearance risk in something they posted, a person they should meet.
- **Never as a set-up.** Help that is visibly a prelude to an ask is a cost, not a
  gift, and people can tell.
- **Beyond our lane, by referral.** If the help they need isn't ours, the help is the
  introduction.

### When we introduce people

An introduction is the highest-value act in this strategy and the easiest to spend
badly.

- **Both sides want it.** Ask each side privately first; never a cold double-opt-out.
- **We can vouch for both.** An introduction is a loan of our reputation to each
  person; we only lend what we have.
- **There's a reason now** — a job, a hire, a problem — not "you two should know each
  other."
- **We step back.** After the introduction, the relationship is theirs. We don't hover.
- **Aim for one a week**, and keep a ledger of who was introduced to whom, because the
  graph of introductions is the network's real shape.

### Creating goodwill before opportunity exists

The compounding mechanism, stated plainly: **goodwill is deposited in small, specific,
unrequested acts over years, and withdrawn only by someone else's initiative.**

- Every Warm relationship carries a positive balance before we receive anything.
- The quarterly act of generosity (Stage 4) is aimed at people who cannot pay us.
- Referrals we send out are logged like revenue; they are the earliest leading
  indicator that the network is real.
- Public credit — naming the editor, the composer, the producer (with consent) —
  costs nothing and is remembered for years.

### The scoreboard

Vanity metrics are banned (Constitution §4.7). These are the numbers that answer the
measure at the top of this document:

| Indicator | What it proves |
|---|---|
| **Unprompted inbound** — briefs, questions, introductions that arrive without a touch from us in the prior 90 days | The relationship survives our silence. The only number that matters. |
| **Introductions made vs. received** (aim to make more than we receive for the first two years) | We are a node, not a leaf. |
| **Seats filled at Warm or above**, out of the hundred | Coverage of the seats that decide. |
| **Goodwill ledger balance** — acts given minus acts received, per person | Whether we are building or spending. |
| **Referrals sent** | The network runs through us in both directions. |
| **"Asked for advice" count** — people who came to us as an authority | The Stage 5 thesis is landing. |
| **Dormant Clients reconnected** | The loop closes on the people who already paid us. |
| **Win/loss on relationship-sourced work** (mandatory capture, per the Constitution) | The relationships convert, and we learn from the ones that don't. |

Reviewed monthly against the hundred, quarterly against this document.

## The first ninety days, from zero

Not a campaign — a starting posture that makes every later stage possible.

1. **Fill the first thirty seats with evidence**, weighted to editors, counsel, peers
   and Southeast agency founders — the people a stranger can approach with a question.
2. **Show up in three Tier A rooms** (an AAF chapter, an AICP or film-commission
   event, a post-house circle) as a guest, not a member. Volunteer for one thing.
3. **Put the clearance certificate and licence in front of three business-affairs
   people** for critique. Their edits improve the product and start the relationships.
4. **Make six introductions** and send two referrals out. Log them.
5. **Book one ad-school session.**
6. **Record every one of these in the graph**, so the operating system starts
   proposing the next action from real evidence rather than from a founder's memory.

If, at the end of ninety days, one person has sent us a question or an introduction
we didn't prompt, the strategy is working. If the only inbound is from our own
posting, it isn't yet — and the answer is more rooms and more usefulness, never more
posts.

# Chordential — The Messaging Bible

*Author: Chief Messaging Officer. Session 2 of the brand programme. Date: 2026-09-08.*

*This document is the source of truth for every sentence Chordential publishes:
website copy, sales conversations, discovery calls, LinkedIn, social, presentations,
investor conversations, client email, product copy, campaigns. It contains no copy.
It contains the language system copy is made from. If it disappeared tomorrow,
another team should be able to rebuild Chordential's voice from this alone.*

## 1 · Stage 1 — What the brand record says, distilled

### 1.1 The central ideas (in order of weight)

1. **Original music for campaigns, composed, cleared, delivered.** The whole business
   in one clause. Three verbs, one object. Everything else elaborates one of the verbs.
2. **Procurement-grade.** Every artifact that reaches a client must read as though it
   came from a company ten times the size. The paperwork is the handshake. The
   certainty is what the buyer is paying for; the music rides along.
3. **Human-made, always.** No AI composes here. Software organises, documents,
   remembers, proposes. A person writes every note and a person presses every
   decision button. *The machine proposes, the human disposes.*
4. **Honesty is a hard constraint.** No invented clients, no borrowed logos, no
   numbers that are not live or labelled. An empty field says *not yet observed*,
   never a guess. Defer what cannot be done well and say so.
5. **Compression is the felt value.** *Manage the campaign; the music is handled.*
   Fewer tabs, fewer decisions carried in the head, nothing re-improvised.
6. **Chosen, not bidding.** Creators are selected for craft, briefed with real scoped
   work, paid promptly. The supply side is a constitutional promise, not a talent pool.
7. **The business compounds.** Every job leaves the studio sharper: what was quoted,
   what it cost, how many rounds, who buys what from whom. This is the investor story
   and the long-term story. It is not the client story.
8. **Warm. Precise. Made.** The visual identity's three words are also the verbal
   register. Warm light on real materials; restraint is the flex.

### 1.2 What deserves repetition

Repeat these until they are boring to us and obvious to the market:

- The three verbs: **composed, cleared, delivered.**
- **Original** (as in written from a blank page for this campaign).
- **Cleared** and the physical evidence of clearing: the **certificate**, the **cue
  sheet**, the **chain of title**, **on file**.
- **Human-made** and the reason it matters: only human-authored music can be owned,
  licensed, and defended.
- **The room**: one link, one place, every note stays with the music.
- **Bounded rounds** (controlled variation): direction → lock → versions, not a spiral.
- **Say what was assumed.** Every range, every estimate, every schedule names what it
  rests on.

### 1.3 What should never be repeated

- **Claims of scale, clients, or awards we do not have.** The record forbids it and it
  is the fastest way to lose the buyer this brand exists for.
- **The moat.** The data flywheel, the buyer↔creator graph, the estimation actuals —
  these are why the business gets better, not what the client buys. They belong to
  investor and internal conversations only.
- **The internal machinery.** Queues, engines, pipelines, qualification, scoring,
  tiers, alerts. A client sees a studio that is impossibly organised, never the
  control room.
- **"AI" as a selling word.** We are not an AI company. AI appears in our language
  only to say what it does *not* do here, and to explain the market fact that makes
  human authorship valuable.
- **Any word that positions us beside a library, a marketplace, or a generator.**
  See §3.

### 1.4 Language that feels premium vs generic (from the record)

| Premium (the record's own register) | Generic (what the record rejects) |
|---|---|
| composed for this campaign · written for the picture | content · tracks · music assets |
| cleared · on file · chain of title · certified | licensed · royalty-free · hassle-free |
| the room · the take · a note · a round · a direction | the platform · the dashboard · feedback loop |
| a planning range · a creative fee · a licence fee | pricing that works for you · flexible packages |
| we reply with an approach and a price range | get a free quote · book a demo |
| chosen · roster · crew · the composer's name | talent · vendors · resources · freelancers |
| everything arrives together | seamless end-to-end delivery |
| not yet observed · illustrative · demonstration | trusted by · industry-leading · proven |

### 1.5 Words that create trust, words that create confusion

**Trust** comes from words that can be checked: *certificate, cue sheet, signed,
on file, 24-bit / 48 kHz, time-aligned, named, dated, version, illustrative,
demonstration, invented brand.* Trust also comes from admitting a limit: *a planning
range, not a quote* · *we do not file a visual code because nothing here knows
whether a performer appears on camera.* The record's honesty rule is a messaging
asset: the studio that says what it does not know is the studio you believe about
the rest.

**Confusion** comes from three sources. Category words that point at the wrong shelf
(*sync, licensing platform, marketplace, library*). Software words leaking into a
music conversation (*pipeline, qualification, workspace, OS*). And borrowed
sincerity (*seamless, world-class, passionate*), which tells the buyer nothing and
signals that we have nothing specific to say.

## 2 · Stage 2 — The Messaging Framework

Every message below is defined seven ways: the idea, the audience, the emotional
objective, the proof, when to use it, when not to. Messages are ideas, not copy.
The wording of each will be written by a later session; the *content* of each is
fixed here.

### 2.1 Core Message

**CM · Original music for campaigns, composed, cleared, delivered — by a studio that
hands it over the way a procurement team wants it.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Idea** | Chordential writes original music for commercial campaigns, with human composers, and delivers it with the paperwork and organisation that lets a buyer ship it without a second thought. The certainty is the product; the music is the craft that rides inside it. |
| **Audience** | Everyone. This is the sentence every other message is traceable to. |
| **Emotional objective** | Relief. *Someone finally does the whole thing, properly.* |
| **Proof** | The Clearance Certificate; the cue sheet; the named chain of title; the delivery package with its naming convention and rollout map; the room where every note stays with the music. All are real, shipped surfaces. |
| **Use** | First contact of any kind. The opening of any page, deck, call, or email where the reader does not yet know us. |
| **Do not use** | As the *only* thing said to someone who already knows us. Repeating the core message to a warm contact reads as a brochure. Move to the supporting message that matches their concern. |

### 2.2 Supporting Messages

**SM1 · The certainty is what you are buying.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Idea** | Legal flags music; campaigns stall; files go missing at 11pm before air. Chordential's job is to remove those events. Original, documented, delivery-ready music means nothing to clear but ours, and nothing to chase. |
| **Audience** | Agency producers, business affairs, marketing ops, brand managers. Anyone who has been the person legal called. |
| **Emotional objective** | Safety without the smell of a legal department. |
| **Proof** | Chain of title (every contributor signs to us before a note leaves the room); no samples, no third-party masters; the certificate's cross-channel and paid-spend licence; the safelist against Content ID claims. |
| **Use** | Discovery calls, proposals, the "why us" moment, objection handling ("we use a library"). |
| **Do not use** | With phrasing that promises legal outcomes. The record is explicit: say *documented, original, delivery-ready*, never *litigation-proof*. Never say *guaranteed* outside the certificate's own defined terms. |

**SM2 · Written for this campaign, by a person whose name is on the cue sheet.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Idea** | Every score is composed for the picture and the brief it serves, from a blank page. It is not stock, not a catalogue pull, not generated. The composer is named. |
| **Audience** | Creative directors, executive producers, music supervisors. People whose filter for pretenders is instant. |
| **Emotional objective** | Respect. *These people care about craft as much as we do.* |
| **Proof** | Demonstrations labelled as demonstrations (AURORA, Vance Athletic, Northwind); the direction → lock → version record; the credit line on every cue. |
| **Use** | Anywhere craft is being judged. The work section, the method, the studio, creative conversations. |
| **Do not use** | To claim a style, a sound, or a "signature." The record does not define a house sound; the brand's signature is the process and the paperwork, not a genre. Never used to disparage libraries; they are *filler*, in the record's word, and orthogonal, not the enemy. |

**SM3 · One room. Every note stays with the music.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Idea** | The client has one link that never changes. They play the take, mark the exact second, say what they heard. The note is priced before a composer sees it, so a comment never silently becomes a scope change. Everything that happened is still there next campaign. |
| **Audience** | Agency producers, brand managers, anyone who has run a review over email threads. |
| **Emotional objective** | Order. The feeling of a studio that is impossibly organised. |
| **Proof** | The review portal with timecoded notes; the priced-notes rule (conform / revision / out of scope, stated before work begins); the durable client link; the kickoff handoff. |
| **Use** | Second-contact messaging, onboarding, the first project, product copy inside the room itself. |
| **Do not use** | As software marketing. The room is *how we work with you*, not a feature list. Do not name it a platform, portal, dashboard, or workspace in client-facing language; call it the room. |

**SM4 · Bounded rounds, not a revision spiral.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Idea** | Direction options first; one chosen, the rest parked; a creative lock; then a defined revision window from which every cutdown derives. Scope is agreed before it is spent. |
| **Audience** | Executive producers, production companies, agency producers. People who own the margin and the calendar. |
| **Emotional objective** | Control. The revision conversation will not eat the schedule. |
| **Proof** | The named versions on every delivery (v1 Direction, v2 Direction Lock, v3 Final Approved); the revision policy stated in the agreement text; the round ledger. |
| **Use** | Proposals, kickoff, the moment a client asks "how many rounds." |
| **Do not use** | As a restriction pitch. The frame is *clarity*, never *limits*. Never *unlimited revisions* (a competitor word the record rejects) and never *strict* or *capped* in client language; say *bounded* and *defined*. |

**SM5 · The number comes with its reasons.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Idea** | A creative fee (what the work costs to make well) plus a licence fee (where it runs, for how long, how exclusively). What the client said their budget is never sets the price; it tells us whether we fit. Every assumption the number rests on is named on the same page. |
| **Audience** | Everyone who signs. Brand managers especially, who "don't know what music should cost." |
| **Emotional objective** | Confidence. The buyer can defend the number upward without us in the room. |
| **Proof** | The itemised derivation on the proposal; the planning range on the public page labelled *not a quote*; the scope evidence markers (stated vs assumed). |
| **Use** | The planning range, the proposal, the commercial review, pricing objections. |
| **Do not use** | To compete on cheapness, ever. And never quote a number in a channel that cannot show its derivation (a social post, a headline). |

**EM1 · Manage the campaign. The music is handled.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Idea** | The Constitution's customer promise: Chordential removes the work of managing music so the buyer can stay on the campaign. |
| **Audience** | Agency producers, brand managers, EPs. |
| **Emotional objective** | Lightness. One fewer thing carried in the head. |
| **Proof** | Everything arriving together; the kickoff that asks for the picture and the deposit and nothing else; a client action that can be taken carries the link that takes it. |
| **Use** | Relationship stages after the first project; the invitation to return; retainer conversations. |
| **Do not use** | Before we have earned it with that buyer. To a stranger it is a claim; to a client it is a description. |

**EM2 · Nothing here was put on screen by accident.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Idea** | The identity's *Made* principle, spoken: restraint, care, craft you can feel at close range. |
| **Audience** | Creative directors, EPs, anyone who lives inside beautiful work. |
| **Emotional objective** | Kinship. They recognise their own standard. |
| **Proof** | Demonstrated, never claimed: the front door, the delivery package, the certificate under a reading lamp. |
| **Use** | The experience itself. This message is almost never *said*; it is shown. When spoken, it is spoken about the work, not about us. |
| **Do not use** | As self-description ("we obsess over details"). The moment restraint is announced it is gone. |

**EM3 · You are chosen here, not bidding.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Idea** | The supply-side promise: real briefs, clean terms, prompt payment, your name on the work, first look on fit work, a personal reel review. |
| **Audience** | Composers, sound designers, arrangers, mixers, supervisors. |
| **Emotional objective** | Dignity. |

### 2.4 Operational Messages

These describe *how* working with us goes. They are educational and plain.

**OM1 · Start with the story.** Every campaign begins with understanding the film, the
brand, the audience, the constraints. The first conversation is about their world, not
ours. Use in first-touch and discovery. Do not use as a pitch for "our process."

**OM2 · Clarity before creation.** Everyone leaves the call with the same understanding
of what is being made, what is fixed, what is open. The Discovery Summary is that
understanding, in writing, and it is what gets signed. Use at proposal. Do not use to
imply the client must do homework; the studio carries the writing.

**OM3 · The method is a signal chain.** Brief → Direction → Composition → Controlled
Variation → Clearance → Delivery. Six stages, each with one promise. Use wherever the
process is explained. Do not add stages, rename them, or expose the internal
sub-stages (kickoff gates, court state, round ledgers) to a client.

**OM4 · Everything arrives together.** Files, documents, approvals, supporting assets,
assembled in one package: manifest, asset map, naming convention, rollout map, cue
sheet, rights certificate, stems at 24-bit / 48 kHz time-aligned to the master. Use at
delivery and in the proof section. Do not list specifications in a first-contact
message; the list is proof, not an opener.

**OM5 · Terms are producer-voiced.** The agreement reads the way a producer would
explain it, generated from the actual scope, and the text the client reads is the
exact text the signature covers. Use at commercial review. Do not describe terms as
"simple" or "friendly"; say what they are.

### 2.5 Proof Messages

Proof messages are the only messages permitted to carry numbers, and every number
carries its date and source. The honesty rule governs: no borrowed credibility.

**PM1 · Only human-authored music can be owned.** The US Copyright Office (January
2025) and a federal appeals court (March 2025) held that AI-only output cannot be
copyrighted, and so cannot be cleanly licensed, insured, or defended. Use to explain
why human authorship is a procurement fact, not a taste. Do not use to attack AI
music companies by name, and re-verify the citation before each use; the record
flags a pivotal ruling expected in 2026.

**PM2 · The cross-channel licence trap is real.** Platform in-app libraries cover
native posts only; repost cross-channel or put paid spend behind it and the blanket
licence dies. Content ID claims follow. Use in education content and discovery. Do
not use as fear; use as a fact the buyer probably already half-knows.

**PM3 · What arrives is checkable.** The certificate names every contributor, their
role, their PRO, the certified version and the date. The naming convention is printed
on the package. Use everywhere proof is asked for. This is the proof message that
replaces client logos.

**PM4 · Our demonstrations are labelled demonstrations.** AURORA, Vance Athletic,
Northwind are invented brands, presented honestly as capability demonstrations. Use
whenever work is shown. Never unlabelled.

**PM5 · The number sits where the market sits.** The pricing research puts a bespoke
:30 at roughly $2,000–25,000 and a published music-house entry package at $10,000.
Use *internally* and in a discovery call when a buyer needs an anchor. Do not publish
competitor prices; do not publish our own outside a page that shows the derivation.

**PM6 · Early pilots, honestly framed.** Until real client quotes exist, the
testimonial slot says so. Real quotes replace the frame as they exist, named and
approved. Use as the standing rule for any social-proof surface.

### 2.6 Transformation Messages

**TM1 · From "is it cleared?" to "it's on file."** Before: a bare mp3, a murky
licence, a legal flag, a stalled campaign. After: a certificate, a cue sheet, a chain
of title, and nothing to chase. Audience: producers and brand managers. Use in case
narratives and discovery. Do not use with an invented "before" client.

**TM2 · From managing music to managing the campaign.** Before: chasing files,
versions, composers, approvals. After: one room, one link, everything arriving
together. Audience: agency producers, EPs. Use after a first project or in a
retainer conversation.

**TM3 · From guessing the budget to defending the number.** Before: "we guessed and
left money on the table," or "we lost on price." After: a creative fee and a licence
fee, itemised, with every assumption named. Audience: brand managers, agency owners.
Use at proposal.

**TM4 · For creators: from bidding to being briefed.** Before: spec work, two hundred
bidders, unpaid pitches. After: chosen for craft, scoped and paid, named on the work.
Audience: creators. Use in recruiting.

### 2.7 Future Vision Messages

These are for investors, partners, and the internal team. They are not client
messages, and the record says why: the moat is the reason the service keeps getting
better, not what the client buys.

**FV1 · The studio is instance zero.** Chordential is a real business run on ChordOS,
with real money at stake. The pattern is proven here before it is generalised.
Audience: investors, advisors. Proof: the live product, the dogfood loop, the phase
ladder A→B→C. Do not use with clients.

**FV2 · The durable asset is the intelligence the business manufactures by
operating.** Qualification labels, estimation actuals, win/loss, revision counts by
segment, the buyer↔creator graph. Anyone can build a scraper in a weekend; nobody
else has this record. Audience: investors. Do not oversell its maturity: the record
is explicit that day-one advantage is curation, speed and priors, and the compounding
dataset is the *future* moat. Say which one you are describing.

**FV3 · The operating layer between creative demand and creative supply.** The
long-term direction: a graph across which buyers find vetted creators and get work
cleared, delivered, documented. Audience: investors, the team. Do not claim it exists.
Do not use "marketplace"; the record rejects becoming Upwork.

**FV4 · Generalisation is earned, never assumed.** Music is the crucible, not a
limitation to escape. Audience: the team, advisors who ask "why not every creative
vertical." Use to answer the question; never as an ambition statement to a client.

## 3 · Stage 3 — The Chordential Vocabulary

### 3.1 Words we use

| Word | Why |
|---|---|
| **original** | The record's first adjective. It is checkable (written from a blank page for this campaign) and it is the legal basis of ownership. |
| **composed · cleared · delivered** | The three verbs. They are the method in three words and they are all past-participle promises: done, not planned. |
| **score / cue / take / version** | Working words from the craft. They tell a creative buyer we are from their world. |
| **the room** | One link, one place, for client, creator and studio. Warm, physical, and it is literally what the product is called. |
| **a note** | What a client leaves on the music. Never "feedback," never "comment." A note is small, specific, and stays with the take. |
| **a round · a direction · a lock** | The revision language. A direction is a choice; a lock is a decision; a round is a bounded unit of change. |
| **certificate · cue sheet · chain of title · on file** | The proof nouns. Each names a physical thing the buyer can hold or forward to legal. |
| **stems · cutdowns · the package** | What arrives. Concrete, countable, standard. |
| **creative fee · licence fee · planning range** | The three money words. Two fees, and a range that is honest about being a range. |
| **scope** | What is agreed before it is spent. Used instead of "deliverables" wherever a person is reading, not a checklist. |
| **composer · sound designer · mixer · supervisor** | Named disciplines, never "talent." The person's name goes with the discipline whenever it can. |
| **roster · crew** | The people we work with. *Roster* for the standing group, *crew* for a project. Never "pool." |
| **chosen** | The supply-side promise in one word. |
| **procurement-grade** | Internal and investor register, and permitted in client copy when speaking to business affairs. It names the standard without naming a client. |
| **studio** | What we are. Not agency, not house, not company, not platform. |
| **session** | The front door's metaphor: a visit is attending a session. Used for the experience, not for meetings ("a call," not "a session," when it is a call). |
| **observed · not yet observed · illustrative · demonstration** | The honesty markers. Every claim carries one when its status is not obvious. |
| **assumed · stated** | Scope evidence. Anything the number rests on is one or the other, and says which. |
| **handoff** | The delivery moment as a human act, not a transfer. |
| **campaign** | The buyer's unit of work. We score campaigns. We do not "create content." |

### 3.3 Industry jargon we replace

| Jargon | Problem | Chordential says |
|---|---|---|
| **Sync licence** | Names catalogue placement; buyers hear "existing track." | *the licence*: where it runs, for how long, how exclusively |
| **Buyout / all-in / perpetual** | Three words for one idea, each with a different legal shadow. | *you own the master outright* (when true), else the licence terms spelled out |
| **MFN / master + publishing split** | Music-industry accounting the buyer should not have to learn. | *writer and publisher shares are accounted separately, as a PRO accounts them* — said only on the certificate, where it is precise |
| **Work-for-hire** | Keep it (it is the legal term) but never alone. | *every contributor signs to us before a note leaves the room; the chain of title is on the certificate* |
| **PRO** | Unexplained acronym. | *performance rights organisation (ASCAP, BMI, SESAC)* on first use |
| **Content ID** | Keep it (buyers know it), explain it once. | *the automatic claim system on YouTube; our music is safelisted so it never claims against your channel* |
| **Deliverables** | Checklist word. | *what arrives* · *the package* (in prose); *deliverables manifest* is fine as a document title |
| **SOW / statement of work** | Procurement's word. | *scope* · *the agreement* |
| **RFP / brief-out / pitch** | Bidding words. We answer briefs; we do not pitch. | *the brief* · *tell us about the project* |
| **Revision rounds (unlimited / capped)** | The industry's argument, on either side. | *bounded rounds* · *a defined revision window* |
| **Mood / temp track / reference** | Keep them; they are the creative's words. | (unchanged; these are craft words, not jargon) |
| **Turnaround** | Delivery-service word. | *the schedule* · *delivered by* (with a date) |
| **Cutdown** | Keep it. Standard and precise. | (unchanged) |
| **Stems** | Keep it. Say the format once: 24-bit / 48 kHz, time-aligned. | (unchanged) |
| **Sonic branding / audio logo / mnemonic** | Three names for one product. | *a sonic signature* (the record's product name) |

### 3.4 Competitor language we refuse to copy

| Their line | Who says it | Why we refuse it |
|---|---|---|
| "Unlimited music for creators" · "one subscription" | Libraries (Epidemic, Artlist) | We are not a catalogue and not a subscription. Volume is never a success metric. |
| "Find the perfect track" · "search millions of songs" | Libraries, sync marketplaces | Presumes the music already exists. Ours does not, until it is written for you. |
| "Royalty-free" | Libraries | Waives royalties, not copyright; the record calls this the trap, not the promise. |
| "Generate a song in seconds" · "AI music for your brand" | Generators (Suno, Udio, Soundraw) | Not copyrightable, not ownable, not ours. We never stand on that shelf, even to mock it. |
| "Hire top composers" · "vetted freelancers" · "post a job" | Marketplaces (Upwork, SoundBetter) | Race-to-bottom labour matching. The record's first drift to refuse. |
| "Music vendor" · "preferred supplier" | Procurement lists | Interchangeable. We are chosen, and so are our composers. |
| "Music licensing platform" · "clearance made easy" | Sync-tech (Lickd, Songtradr arms) | A platform for other people's music. We clear our own, by making it. |
| "Trusted by [logos]" · "as used by" | Everyone | Fabricated on our site until it is true; and the record ruled it out. |
| "Fast, affordable, high quality — pick three" | Every service page | Cheapness is the wrong axis and the joke is fifteen years old. |
| "Your creative partner" · "we're an extension of your team" | Agencies and houses | Says nothing checkable. Our partnership is a room and a certificate, not a sentence. |
| "Award-winning" · "Awwwards-grade" | Studios, and our own experience spec | Internal ambition, never external claim. The buyer is an EP; awards are not proof to them. |

**The four the brief asked about, answered:**

- Instead of **music vendor**: *a music studio* (the one you chose).
- Instead of **composer marketplace**: *a roster, chosen for craft*.
- Instead of **music licensing platform**: *original music, cleared and on file*.
- Instead of **AI music company**: *human-made, always; software beneath the studio,
  never in the music*.

## 4 · Stage 4 — Voice & Tone

### 4.1 How Chordential speaks

**Warm. Precise. Made.** The identity's three words are the voice's three words. In
prose that means:

| Axis | Where we sit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Confident vs arrogant** | Confident. We state what we do, in declaratives, without hedging and without superlatives. We never rank ourselves against a named peer, and we never say we are the best at anything. | Our buyer cannot be impressed by claims, only by taste demonstrated under their nose. Arrogance is a claim. Confidence is a description. |
| **Technical vs accessible** | Precise where precision protects the buyer (rights, formats, versions, money); plain everywhere else. Every technical term is explained on first use in the same sentence, never in a glossary. | Procurement-grade means a business-affairs reader finds the exact fact; a creative reader is never made to feel outside the room. |
| **Warm vs corporate** | Warm. First person plural, second person address. A studio speaking to a person. Never chummy, never exclamatory, never "hey." | The room has a temperature. Corporate voice is cold light; ours is the lamp just off-frame. |
| **Elegant vs clever** | Elegant. No puns, no wordplay, no winks. Wit lives in restraint and in exact observation. | Cleverness asks for applause. Elegance asks for nothing. The Made principle: nothing on the page that was not put there on purpose. |
| **Educational vs promotional** | Educational. We explain how clearance works, why human authorship is a legal fact, what a cue sheet does. We do not pitch. When we make an offer it is specific: a scoped call, a written offer, a deposit. | The record's playbook closes on one of three concrete asks, never "let's stay in touch." Teaching earns the ask. |
| **Present vs future** | Present tense, past-participle promises (*composed, cleared, delivered*). The future belongs to investor conversations. | A client buys what exists. |
| **Certain vs honest** | Honest wins. Where we do not know, we say *not yet observed* or *a range, not a quote*. | The studio that admits a limit is believed about the rest. |

### 4.2 The sentence rules

1. **One idea per sentence.** Short does not mean clipped; a sentence beats a label
   with a colon.
2. **Verbs carry the meaning.** Composed, cleared, delivered, signed, filed, named,
   arrived. Adjectives are suspects; each must be checkable.
3. **Name the thing.** "The certificate," not "our documentation." "24-bit / 48 kHz,"
   not "high quality." "AURORA," not "a leading outdoor brand."
4. **Numbers only with their reasons.** A number without its derivation or its date
   does not ship.
5. **Second person for the buyer, first person plural for the studio, a name for the
   composer.** Never "clients," "users," or "talent" in address.
6. **The honesty marker travels with the claim.** *Illustrative. Demonstration.
   Invented brand. Not a quote. Not yet observed.* In the same sentence or the same
   line, never in a footnote.
7. **Explain a term the first time, in the sentence.** Never assume the buyer knows
   what a cue sheet is; never make them feel stupid for not knowing.
8. **No exclamation marks.** No emoji in prose. No rhetorical questions in copy (they
   are fine in a conversation).
9. **Cut the last sentence.** If it restates, offers, or reassures, it goes.

### 4.3 Calibration samples

These are voice illustrations for calibrating a writer's ear. They are not copy and
must not be published as written.

**Good messaging** (warm, precise, made):

> Every person who plays on your score signs to us before a note leaves the room.
> The certificate names them, their role, and the version it certifies.

> This is a planning range, not a quote. It is here so you can check we fit the line
> before we talk.

> The files are named so anyone on the campaign can find any cut in seconds. The
> convention is printed on the package.

*Why they work:* each sentence is a checkable fact; each names a thing; each admits
its own scope. Nothing asks to be admired.

**Weak messaging** (true, but soft):

> We take rights really seriously and make sure everything is properly cleared.

> We'll work with you to find the right sound for your campaign.

*Why they fail:* "seriously" and "properly" are unverifiable; "work with you to find"
describes every studio alive. Nothing here could be forwarded to legal.

**Generic messaging** (could belong to any B2B tool):

> Chordential is a seamless, end-to-end music solution for modern brands, powered by
> cutting-edge technology and a passion for creativity.

*Why it fails:* six of the record's forbidden words in one sentence; no verb that
does anything; nothing a buyer could check or a competitor could not also say.

**Overwritten messaging** (clever, breathless, decorated):

> In a world drowning in AI noise, we are the last lighthouse of human sound, a
> sanctuary where every note is born, not generated, and every campaign finds its
> voice at last.

*Why it fails:* announced restraint is gone; the metaphor competes with the fact; it
disparages a category the record says never to stand beside; and it says "born," which
is an adjective in disguise. The same content in our voice: *Every note here is
written by a person. Their name is on the cue sheet.*

### 4.4 Tone by situation

The voice is constant. The tone moves.

| Situation | Tone shift | Held constant |
|---|---|---|
| First contact (stranger) | Quietest. Lead with their world. One ask. | No claims, no logos, honesty markers on every demo. |
| Discovery call | Curious. Questions about how they source music and what happens when legal asks. | We name the pain back to them in their words, not ours. |
| Proposal / commercial review | Most precise. Producer-voiced terms, itemised number, every assumption named. | No "simple," no "friendly." Precision is the warmth here. |
| The room (in production) | Concierge. Short, present-tense status; who holds the ball; what happens next. | Never software vocabulary. |
| Delivery | Ceremony. The one place the register rises: the certificate, the seal, the handoff. | Still no superlatives. The ceremony is in the paper, not the adjectives. |
| A refusal or a limit | Plain and immediate. What we cannot do, why, and what we can. | Never apologetic filler; never "unfortunately." |
| Creator-facing | Direct and respectful. Terms first, then the work. | Never "opportunity" as a euphemism for unpaid. |
| Investor-facing | Analytical. The moat, the phases, the honest TAM. | The honesty rule applies harder here, not softer. |

## 5 · Stage 5 — Audience Messaging

Six frameworks. Each names what the audience cares about, what keeps them awake, the
language that earns and loses trust, and the proof that matters most. Messages are
referenced by their §2 codes so every audience traces back to the framework.

### 5.1 Executive Producers (production companies, film and commercial)

- **They care about:** margin and calendar. Music is usually the last line item and the
  last thing sourced. They own the project budget.
- **What keeps them awake:** a composer who is late, a cut that changes after the score
  is written, a licence that does not cover where the client now wants to run it, and a
  music bill that arrives after the budget was locked.
- **Language that earns trust:** *scope, schedule, bounded rounds, delivered by, the
  licence covers, the number and its reasons.* Talking in their unit of time (air date,
  picture lock). Naming what happens when the picture changes (the parked cut, the
  stated offset).
- **Language that loses credibility:** *passion, sound design philosophy, our
  signature style, unlimited revisions, fast.* Anything that sounds like a composer
  pitching rather than a studio delivering. Any number without a derivation.
- **Proof that matters most:** the versioned delivery record (Direction → Lock →
  Final), the revision policy in the agreement text, and the rollout map showing the
  right cut in the right place on air date.
- **Messages:** SM4, SM5, OM3, OM4, TM2. Never FV*.

### 5.2 Agency Producers

- **They care about:** getting the spot out without a fire. They are the person legal
  calls, the person the client calls, and the person who chases the file at 11pm.
- **What keeps them awake:** "is this actually cleared?", a Content ID claim on the
  client's channel, `final_final_v2`, and a creative director who wants one more pass
  the day before air.
- **Language that earns trust:** *on file, certificate, cue sheet, one link, every
  note stays with the music, nothing to chase, everything arrives together.* The
  studio speaking as an organised colleague, not a vendor.
- **Language that loses credibility:** *seamless, platform, portal, AI-powered, we'll
  make it easy.* Anything that sounds like a tool they will have to learn or a promise
  they have heard from every library.
- **Proof that matters most:** the room (timecoded notes, one link that never
  changes), the naming convention printed on the package, the safelist against
  Content ID, the certificate they can forward to legal without a cover note.
- **Messages:** SM1, SM3, OM4, EM1, TM1, TM2. This is the audience the customer
  promise (EM1) was written for.

### 5.3 Creative Directors

- **They care about:** the idea and whether the music serves it. Taste, at close
  range. They can tell craft from noise in one listen, and they know when a studio is
  performing craft rather than doing it.
- **What keeps them awake:** music that is generic, music that fights the picture, a
  process that turns creative conversation into a change-order argument, and being
  handed a library pull dressed as original.
- **Language that earns trust:** *written for the picture, composed for this
  campaign, the direction, the take, mark the moment you mean, creative judgment is
  still the most important instrument.* Craft words used correctly. The composer's
  name.
- **Language that loses credibility:** *content, asset, solution, deliverable,
  ROI, efficiency.* Any hint that the music is a commodity or that the process exists
  to constrain them. Superlatives about our own taste.
- **Proof that matters most:** the demonstrations, labelled and answering a real
  brief; the direction options (thesis, chosen, parked); the room's notes staying with
  the music so creative conversation moves the work forward.
- **Messages:** SM2, SM3, EM2, OM1, OM2. Say SM4 only as clarity, never as a limit.

### 5.4 Brand Managers (in-house marketing and creative teams)

- **They care about:** confidence and cost control. They do not know what music
  should cost or whom to trust, and they are often paying agency markup for both.
- **What keeps them awake:** overpaying, underpaying and getting burned, a licence
  that dies the moment the video goes paid, and being asked by legal for paperwork
  they do not have.
- **Language that earns trust:** *a planning range, not a quote; the creative fee and
  the licence fee; where it runs, for how long; you own the master outright; every
  assumption named.* Teaching them how music is priced before asking for anything.
- **Language that loses credibility:** *premium, bespoke, luxury* (they hear
  "expensive"); *cheap, affordable* (they hear "risky"); any acronym unexplained;
  *sync*.
- **Proof that matters most:** the itemised derivation on the proposal; the
  cross-channel and paid-spend licence written into the certificate; the fact that
  their stated budget is a check, never the price.
- **Messages:** SM1, SM5, PM2, TM3, OM5. Educational tone throughout; this audience is
  won by being taught.

### 5.5 Production Companies (as an organisation, the repeat channel)

- **They care about:** a music partner they can put in front of every client without
  re-vetting, at a predictable price shape, who makes them look organised.
- **What keeps them awake:** reputational exposure when a music supplier fails in
  front of their client; inconsistency between jobs; a partner who is great once and
  unavailable the second time.
- **Language that earns trust:** *the same package every time, the same certificate,
  the same room; retainer; first look; a roster, not one person.* Consistency
  language, and the honesty to say what capacity is.
- **Language that loses credibility:** *scale, unlimited, always available, any
  genre.* Over-claiming capacity is the fastest way to lose a repeat channel; the
  record's playbook says sell one, deliver it flawlessly, then open the next two.
- **Proof that matters most:** the delivery package as a repeatable format (manifest,
  asset map, convention, rollout map); the agreements every contributor signs; the
  named packages (Social Sprint, Campaign, Brand Anthem) as shapes they can quote
  onward.
- **Messages:** OM4, SM4, SM5, EM1, TM2, and PM3 as the repeatability proof. Retainer
  conversations may use EM1 once earned.

### 5.6 Music Supervisors

- **They care about:** clearability, budget, and time. They are drowning in
  submissions and their job is to place the right music with paperwork that survives
  business affairs.
- **What keeps them awake:** a track that clears on Tuesday and does not on
  Thursday; a split that is wrong on the cue sheet; a composer who cannot produce the
  paperwork; a budget that does not survive the first licence conversation.
- **Language that earns trust:** *chain of title, writer and publisher shares
  accounted as a PRO accounts them, certified against version X on date Y, usage
  codes, no samples, no third-party masters.* The exact vocabulary of their job, used
  exactly.
- **Language that loses credibility:** *royalty-free, hassle-free clearance, pre-
  cleared, library.* And any imprecision about rights: one wrong term and they stop
  reading. Never tell a supervisor what a cue sheet is.
- **Proof that matters most:** the certificate's contributor list with roles and
  PROs; the honesty line about the visual code (we never file what we cannot know);
  the stems and their format. This audience reads the paper first and the music
  second.
- **Messages:** SM1 (in its most technical register), PM3, OM4, SM2. Skip EM1 and the
  room's concierge tone; they want the document.

### 5.7 The two audiences the record adds

Not requested, but the record carries them and the vocabulary must not break when it
reaches them.

- **Creators (composers, sound designers, mixers, supervisors as suppliers).** EM3,
  TM4. Language: *chosen, real briefs, clean terms, prompt payment, your name on the
  work, we review every reel personally.* Losing language: *opportunity, exposure,
  community, gig, marketplace.* Proof: the agreements, the payout surface, the reel
  review by a person.
- **Investors and advisors.** FV1–FV4, PM5. Language: *instance zero, the phase
  ladder, the manufactured dataset, the buyer↔creator graph, procurement-grade,
  self-funded.* Losing language: inflated TAM (the record insists on the $650M
  recorded-sync figure and the honest bundle warning), *AI-first*, *marketplace* as
  the near-term claim. Proof: the live product, the dogfood loop, the honesty of the
  numbers.

## 7 · Stage 7 — Narrative Architecture

Chordential tells one story, in one order, in every medium. A page, a deck, a call, a
proposal, and a delivery each tell a *portion* of it, and the portions join.

### 7.1 The spine of the story

> **A campaign needs music it can ship.** Not more music; music it can own, run
> anywhere, and defend. Since 2025 that has been a legal fact, not a preference: only
> music a person wrote can be owned at all.
>
> **So a studio writes it.** For this picture, from a blank page, by a composer with a
> name. Direction first; one chosen; a lock; then a defined window of change from
> which every cut derives.
>
> **And clears it as it goes.** Every contributor signs before a note leaves the room.
> The chain of title is not a search afterwards; it is the way the work was made.
>
> **In one room.** One link. The take, the note at the exact second, the version
> history, the agreement, the deposit. Nothing chased, nothing re-explained.
>
> **Then hands it over the way procurement wants it.** The certificate. The cue sheet.
> The stems, named so anyone can find any cut. The rollout map. Everything at once.
>
> **And remembers.** Next campaign starts with everything this one taught us. The
> studio gets sharper; it does not start over.
>
> **Beneath it is a system we built to run a studio this way.** It proposes; people
> decide. It documents; people compose. That system is how a small studio behaves like
> a company ten times its size, and it is the reason the next job is easier than the
> last.

Every narrative below is one of those seven beats, expanded, in the same order. A
medium may stop early. It may never reorder.

### 7.2 How the company explains itself

Chordential is **a studio**, first and last. The order of introduction is: what we
make (original music for campaigns), what makes it different (cleared and delivered
like procurement wants), how (human composers, a bounded method, one room), and only
then, if asked, what runs beneath it.

The company never introduces itself by its category label. *Music Opportunity
Intelligence* and *the operating system for commercial music procurement* are
internal and investor labels. To a buyer, the category is answered by the three
verbs.

The company never introduces itself by its founder's inbox, its size, or its age. It
introduces itself by its paperwork and its work. A studio that says "small" is asking
for allowances; a studio that hands over a certificate is not.

### 7.3 How to introduce ChordOS

**To a client: as the reason, never as the offer.** ChordOS is mentioned only when a
buyer asks *how* the studio manages to be this organised, and the answer is one
sentence: *we built the system we run on, and it does the remembering and documenting
so the people can do the composing.* It has a name because things that exist have
names. It has no client-facing feature list. The client remembers the surface (the
room, the package), not the software.

**To a creator: as the thing that pays them on time and keeps their name on the
work.** Terms first, then the room they will work in.

**To an investor: as the asset.** ChordOS is the operating system beneath a
creative-service business, with Chordential as instance zero. The story is FV1 → FV2
→ FV3 → FV4, in that order: proven on a real business first, manufacturing the data
by operating, growing into the layer between demand and supply, generalising only
when earned. The analogy the record uses (*Frame.io meets Salesforce, for running a
craft business*) is permitted here and nowhere client-facing.

**Never:** as a product for sale, until Phase B is opened by the CEO. Never as *AI*.
Never with the control-room vocabulary in front of a buyer.

### 7.4 How to explain the process

Six stages, always the same six, always in order: **Brief → Direction → Composition
→ Controlled Variation → Clearance → Delivery.** Each stage carries one promise, and
the promise is a fact about what happens, not an adjective about how well.

- *Brief*: we start with the story; everyone leaves with the same understanding.
- *Direction*: options to picture and brief; one chosen, the rest parked, before full
  production starts.
- *Composition*: written for this campaign, by a named person.
- *Controlled Variation*: bounded rounds; a lock; every cutdown derives from it.
- *Clearance*: every contributor signed before a note leaves; the certificate.
- *Delivery*: everything arrives together, named so anyone can find it.

The process is explained as a **signal chain**, the record's own image: a pulse
travels the chain, each stage lights. This is the one metaphor the process is allowed.
It is not explained as a funnel, a journey, a pipeline, or a workflow.

Internal sub-stages (kickoff gates, court state, the round ledger, phase enums) are
never spoken to a client. The client experiences them as concierge sentences in the
room: who holds the ball, what happens next.

### 7.5 How to explain pricing

Pricing is explained as **two fees and a check**, and it is explained *before* it is
asked for.

1. **A creative fee** is what the work costs to make well: the crew, the hours, the
   instrumentation, the schedule. It is built from the scope, and every assumption
   the scope rests on is named beside the number.
2. **A licence fee** is where the music runs, for how long, and how exclusively. It
   is the part that changes when the campaign grows, and it is written into the
   certificate.
3. **Their budget is a check, not an input.** It tells us whether we fit the line. It
   never sets the price, in either direction.

Before any conversation, the buyer can see **a planning range, labelled not a
quote**, from three answers. The proposal shows the **itemised derivation**. The
objection the record anticipates (a music house that quotes one number, licence
included) is answered with the derivation, never with a discount: *the one number
hides the licence; ours shows it, so you can defend it when the media plan changes.*

Never: compete on cheap; call ourselves premium; quote a number where its reasons
cannot be shown; move a price because a budget was mentioned.

### 7.6 How to explain AI

In three moves, always the same three, always in this order:

1. **What is true in the market.** Most music uploaded today is generated. Music that
   is only generated cannot be copyrighted, so it cannot be owned, licensed, insured,
   or defended. That is the US Copyright Office's position (January 2025), upheld on
   appeal (March 2025). *Re-verify before every use; the record expects further rulings.*
2. **What is true here.** Every note is written by a person. Their name is on the cue
   sheet. Masters are performed by people, never generated. This is not a stance; it is
   the reason the certificate can exist.
3. **What software does here.** The system beneath the studio organises, documents,
   remembers, and proposes. It never composes and never decides. A person presses every
   button that commits us to anything.

Tone: factual, unbothered. We do not fear AI, we do not mock it, and we do not use it
as a villain. We explain a legal fact and describe our practice. The moment the copy
sounds anxious or triumphant it has broken the voice.

Never: *AI-powered*, *AI-free* as a badge, *the last human studio*, or any line that
makes the buyer feel they are choosing a side. They are choosing something they can own.

### 7.7 How to explain original composition

Original means **written for this campaign, from a blank page, by a named composer.**
Three checkable facts. Not a style, not a genre, not a mood.

The explanation always attaches the craft to the picture: composed *for* the film,
the brand, the audience, the constraints. Creative judgment is described as the most
important instrument in the room; the studio's job is to turn complexity into a
direction everyone can hear.

The demonstrations are how original is *shown*: each answers a campaign brief we set
ourselves, and each is labelled as a demonstration with an invented brand. Original
is never claimed with a real client's name we do not have.

The contrast with libraries is stated once, without contempt: a library is filler;
this is yours, owned, with the paperwork legal wants.

### 7.8 How to explain delivery

Delivery is the **ceremony** and the **proof**, and it is described as a physical
handoff: a package, a certificate, a seal.

What arrives, in the order it is listed on the package: the deliverables manifest
(everything contracted, accounted for in one place), the asset map and the naming
convention (so anyone can find any file), the version record (direction, lock, final),
the rights and ownership certificate (grant of rights, usage codes, chain of title,
certified against a named version on a date), mix-ready stems (24-bit / 48 kHz,
time-aligned to the master), the campaign rollout map (every cut mapped to where it
runs), and the final approval certificate.

The honesty lines on the package are part of the message, not exceptions to it: we
never file a visual code because nothing here knows whether a performer appears on
camera; writer and publisher shares are accounted separately, as a PRO accounts them.
A studio that says what it does not claim on the certificate is the studio whose
certificate gets believed.

Delivery is where the register is allowed to rise. It rises in the *paper* (the
seal, the ink, the weight of the stamp), never in the adjectives.

### 7.9 The story, continuous

Read the seven narratives above end to end and they are the seven beats of §7.1:
the campaign's need, the studio that writes, the clearing as it goes, the room, the
handoff, the remembering, and the system beneath. The front door tells beats one
through five. A discovery call tells one, two and five. A proposal tells two, three
and five in their precise register. The room tells four. The package tells five. An
investor deck tells six and seven. No medium tells them out of order, and no medium
invents an eighth.

## 9 · Concerns logged, strategy unchanged

The brief allows disagreement to be documented, not enacted. These are the places
where I would have argued in the room.

1. **Two positionings, one precedence rule.** The June brief's *Music Opportunity
   Intelligence* one-liner and the July Constitution's *procurement-grade music
   studio* describe two different companies to a stranger. I have applied the record's
   own precedence (the Constitution wins) and treated the software positioning as
   Phase B. If the CEO intends the software to be marketed sooner, this Bible needs a
   Phase B chapter, not a rewrite; the vocabulary here is built so ChordOS can step
   forward without the studio's voice changing.

2. **"Guaranteed" and "indemnification" versus "never litigation-proof."** The product
   spec's certificate promises *guaranteed-clearable* and *indemnification*; the sales
   playbook forbids *litigation-proof*. Both stand. My ruling for language: the
   certificate's defined terms may use the certificate's words; prose around it uses
   *documented, original, delivery-ready, we stand behind it*. I would prefer the
   product spec's "guaranteed" be reviewed by counsel before it appears anywhere a
   buyer can quote it back.

3. **"Frame.io meets Salesforce."** The analogy uses two real trademarks. It is
   confined here to investor and internal use. I note that the honesty rule's spirit
   (no borrowed credibility) argues for eventually retiring it even there.

4. **The customer promise says "agencies."** *Chordential removes the work of managing
   music so agencies can stay focused on managing the campaign.* Brand managers and
   production companies are also buyers in the record. I have kept the sentence as
   written (it is load-bearing copy by the Constitution's own description) and mapped
   EM1 to all three audiences; a future amendment might widen the noun.

5. **The experience bible's "Awwwards signature."** An internal ambition that, if it
   leaks into messaging, contradicts the *no awards we do not have* rule and the EP's
   filter for pretenders. Logged so no writer mistakes it for a claim.

6. **Dated market facts.** PM1 and PM2 rest on rulings and statistics with dates and
   confidence flags in the research. The record itself expects a 2026 fair-use ruling.
   Every use of PM1 must re-verify; a Bible cannot make a legal fact permanent.

7. **The category label is buyer-invisible.** *Music Opportunity Intelligence* is
   specific and ownable, as the brief says, and no buyer in the six audiences would
   search for it or recognise it. Right for a category we intend to own in Phase B;
   wrong on any surface a producer reads today. Logged, not changed.

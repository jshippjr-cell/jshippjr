# The Chordential Sales Playbook

*Author: Chief Revenue Officer. Status: v1, 2026-09-08. The sales operating philosophy for
Chordential — the document every future salesperson follows, and the one a new hire could
sell from tomorrow if every salesperson disappeared tonight.*

This playbook **inherits** the Brand Strategy, the Messaging Framework, the Content
Strategy, the Growth Strategy, the Editorial Strategy and the Relationship Strategy. It
does not amend any of them. Where it needs a line of copy, a claim, or a channel, it
defers to them. It owns exactly one thing: **how Chordential sells.**

It sits under the Constitution (`docs/architecture/CONSTITUTION.md`). Where the two seem
to conflict, the Constitution wins and this document is corrected.

It contains no emails, no scripts, no proposals and no prices. Those live elsewhere and are
pointed to where they belong. What it contains is the *thinking* — so that the emails,
calls and proposals a salesperson produces come out the same way whoever produces them.

---

## 0 · Before the stages: what kind of selling this is

### The one question

> **If every salesperson disappeared tomorrow, could a new hire consistently sell
> Chordential the same way by following this playbook?**

Every section below is written to pass that test. Where a section would need a
particular person's charm to work, it has been rewritten until it doesn't.

### The first principle

**Sales begins long before the first meeting, and its purpose is not persuasion.** The
purpose of sales at Chordential is to discover whether Chordential is the right partner
for this piece of work, for this buyer, at this moment. If we are, the discovery makes
that obvious and the client moves forward on their own. If we are not, we say so, and we
say who might be.

This is not a soft position. It is the only sales posture that survives contact with the
buyers we want: agency producers, executive producers, heads of production, brand content
leads and business-affairs people. They live inside beautiful work all day and their
filter for pretenders is instant. They cannot be persuaded. They can only be *shown*, and
the thing they are shown has to be true.

We do not optimise for closing quickly. We optimise for **becoming the obvious choice** —
the studio a producer thinks of before they think of the problem, because the last time
they had the problem we made it go away without drama.

### What we actually sell

The market pays for a **music service**: original, human-made, clearance-certified music
for campaigns. But the buyer's pain is rarely the music. It is **rights risk and delivery
chaos** — a library track with murky clearance, a freelancer who hands over a bare mp3
with no paperwork, a client's legal team asking "is this actually cleared?" three days
before air. So what we sell, in the buyer's own terms, is:

> **Certainty and a painless process. The music rides along.**

Concretely: original music with a signed clearance certificate, a cue sheet, versioned
files, organised delivery, a review room with timecoded notes, and one person to call.
The music is the part they could also buy cheaper elsewhere. The certainty is what they
are paying for. Every conversation is anchored there, not in adjectives about sound.

### The governing rules this playbook honours

These come from the Constitution and are not negotiable in a sale:

1. **The machine proposes, the human disposes.** The OS qualifies, ranks, estimates and
   drafts. A named human decides to pursue, to price, to send, to sign. No automation
   ever commits us to a buyer, and no salesperson tells a buyer that it does.
2. **No AI-generated audio.** Ever. We say so plainly, in copy and in conversation. AI
   may help us organise, document and draft; it is never the composer.
3. **The honesty rule is a hard constraint.** Never imply real client work that did not
   happen. Never invent a capability, a credit, a metric or a logo. Demos use invented
   brands (AURORA, Vance Athletic, HALVARD) and are labelled as demonstrations. What we
   cannot do well, we defer and say so.
4. **Precision over recall.** We pursue few opportunities, well. A salesperson's calendar
   full of the wrong conversations is a failure, not activity.
5. **The loop closes.** Every pursued opportunity gets a win/loss outcome and a reason,
   captured in the OS. A deal that ends without a recorded reason is a deal we learned
   nothing from.

### The claims register

Because the honesty rule is binding, **a salesperson may only make claims that are on the
verified claims list**, maintained by the operator in the capabilities document and the
clearance-certificate copy (one source of truth for legally material text — ADR-0012). A
claim that is *plausible* is not a claim that is *true*. In particular, the sales
simulations in `docs/sales-simulations/` contain lines about insurance, response-time
service levels and session-file archiving that were written to test a call, not to
describe the studio. None of them may be repeated to a buyer unless the operator has
verified them and added them to the register.

The rule in one line: **if you have to check whether we can say it, we cannot say it yet.**

### Who buys, and who decides

The person who feels the pain and the person who writes the cheque are often different
people. The salesperson holds both in mind from the first touch:

| Who | Feels | Decides |
|---|---|---|
| Agency producer / senior producer | Chasing files, versions, "is it cleared?" | Vendor choice under a threshold; runs the job |
| Executive producer / head of production | Margin, schedule, vendor risk on a client | Vendor choice above the threshold; co-signs |
| Creative director | The sound; whether it is right | Creative approval; can send it back |
| Business affairs / legal / procurement | Chain of title, warranties, the MSA, the PO | Whether the paper is acceptable at all |
| Brand-side marketing / content lead | What music should cost, who to trust | The budget line; final approval on the client side |

A sale has not been made until the person who can send it back has seen it and the
person who moves the paper has accepted the paper. Discovery exists partly to find out
who those people are (Stage 3).

## Stage 1 · The buying journey

Twelve stages, from a buyer who has never heard of us to one who sends us their friends.
For each: what the client is thinking, what concerns them, what information they need,
and what confidence they need before they move. Then what our job is, and where in the OS
the stage lives — because the surfaces the client touches *are* the sales process.

The stages the OS already computes (`intro → discovery → brief → commercial → kickoff →
production → delivery → archive`) map onto the middle of this journey. The ends — never
heard of us, and referral — are where a salesperson earns their keep.

### 1.1 Never heard of Chordential

**Thinking.** Nothing about us. They are thinking about their slate, a picture lock that
slipped, a client who said "just use the scratch." Music is a line item they will get to.

**Concerns.** None about us, several about music: the incumbent music house is slammed;
the library subscription didn't cover the paid-media lift last time; a creative director
brought an AI scratch to a presentation and now somebody has to replace it.

**Information they need.** None yet. This stage is not about information. It is about
whether, when the problem next lands on their desk, our name is anywhere near it.

**Confidence to move.** They don't move. We do — but only where the Growth Strategy and
the Relationship Strategy say we may, and only with something specific to *their* work.

**Our job.** Be findable and be specific. The public front door exists so that a producer
who is sent our name can look in five seconds and see craft demonstrated, not claimed.
Outbound, when it happens, is a short personal note that proves we read their brief, with
at most one relevant example and one low-pressure page link — never a brochure (the
first-touch ruling of 2026-06-25 is binding: minimal by default, richness opt-in, the page
is a bonus and never the payload). The OS proposes who and why-today; a human decides
whether to write.

**Where it lives.** Agency intelligence, signals, the outreach composer, the per-lead
first-touch page.

### 1.2 Aware

**Thinking.** "A music studio that leads with clearance. Interesting, or a gimmick?"

**Concerns.** Is this real? Who are they? Have they done anything? Is "procurement-grade"
a filing cabinet that went to business school, or does it mean something I would feel?

**Information they need.** One thing that demonstrates taste and one thing that
demonstrates the paperwork. Not a list of services. A single example that sounds like the
kind of work they do, and a glimpse of what a delivery from us looks like — the
certificate, the cue sheet, the organised package.

**Confidence to move.** "These people care about craft as much as we do" (the Experience
bible's target thought) *and* "the paperwork might actually be the product." Either alone
is insufficient. Craft without paper is every other composer; paper without craft is a
vendor.

**Our job.** Show, don't claim. The front-of-house site and the first-touch page do this.
The salesperson's contribution is restraint: no follow-up that adds pressure, no second
email that adds a track. Awareness ripens on its own or it doesn't.

### 1.3 Interested

**Thinking.** "I have a job coming that this might fit. Or I have a problem I want to
ask them about."

**Concerns.** Will asking commit me to anything? Will they pitch me for an hour? Do they
understand my kind of work, or will I have to educate them?

**Information they need.** What a first conversation is (short, about their work, ends
with a written summary they can correct). What we don't do (we do not generate music with
AI, we do not license existing songs, we do not sell a catalogue). Rough shape of how we
price — *how* it is built, not a number.

**Confidence to move.** That the discovery call is a diagnosis, not a pitch, and that
saying "not for this one" afterwards will be easy.

**Our job.** Make the discovery call cheap to accept and honest about what it is. The
offer is a scoped conversation with a defined length and a defined output. Nothing else is
asked for.

**Where it lives.** The meeting is scheduled in the OS so the notetaker joins; the prep
sheet is generated for the call (`/opportunity/{id}/prep`).

### 1.4 Discovery call

**Thinking.** "Let's see if they get it." Also: "I have forty minutes and a colour session
at half past."

**Concerns.** Being sold to. Being asked for a budget before they've said what the job
is. Being made to feel ignorant about licensing terms. Wasting the time of the people they
brought.

**Information they need.** Surprisingly little from us. They need to feel *understood* —
which is produced by the quality of the questions, not by the quantity of our answers.
They need one thing they didn't know (a single teach moment, chosen for them). And they
need to know what happens next, by when.

**Confidence to move.** "They asked the questions my last vendor didn't, they didn't
assume anything, and I'll have it in writing today." Stage 3 is entirely about producing
that confidence.

**Our job.** Understand the work, the sound, the plan, the money, the people, the paper
and the risk; decide honestly whether we are the right partner; read it back; send the
summary the same day.

**Where it lives.** The notetaker, the capture, Campaign Intelligence, the Discovery
Summary.

### 1.5 Proposal

**Thinking.** "Did they hear me?" Then: "What does it cost, and what am I getting for
it?" Then: "Can I forward this to my head of production without explaining it?"

**Concerns.** A number that doesn't match what they said. Scope that quietly grew or
shrank. Terms buried in boilerplate. Anything that reads as a template with their name
pasted in. A price with no derivation (they will be asked to defend it internally).

**Information they need.** Exactly what will be made, for what use, for how long, where,
exclusive or not, by when, for how much, in what two parts (creative fee and licence
fee), with what included and what optional, and what happens if the picture moves.

**Confidence to move.** "This is what I said, priced in a way I can explain to someone
else, with nothing hidden." Stage 4 is about producing that.

**Our job.** The Discovery Summary **is** the proposal (ADR-0065). It carries scope, fee,
terms and the Agreement block in one deterministic text — what the client reads is what
the signature covers. The salesperson's job is to make sure it says what the client said,
names what was assumed, and prices what was asked for — not what the budget was.

**Where it lives.** The client workspace: the Campaign Brief and the Discovery Summary.

### 1.6 Commercial Review

**Thinking.** "Now the grown-up part. Who signs, how do we pay, what does legal need?"

**Concerns.** Procurement friction: our paper versus their MSA, purchase orders, vendor
onboarding, W-9s, certificates of insurance, payment terms that don't match their cycle.
A deposit they cannot pay because the grant pays on delivery. A finance officer who is in
on Wednesdays.

**Information they need.** The commercial conversation *separated from the creative one*
(principle 7 of the client workspace): scope, deliverables, timeline, pricing, deposit,
payment schedule, terms in a producer's voice, a procurement checklist, and one clear act
of approval.

**Confidence to move.** "Nothing here will surprise my business-affairs person, and if
they need it on our paper, that's a conversation not a fight."

**Our job.** Procurement is *adaptive* (principle 5): we discover how their organisation
buys and produce the artefacts it needs. We do not integrate into their systems; we adapt
to them. Terms are dynamic and readable, never boilerplate. Approval is electronic in the
workspace by default; a third-party signature only when procurement requires it.

**Where it lives.** The Commercial Review, frozen at operator release, approved by the
client in the workspace.

### 1.7 Commitment

**Thinking.** "We're doing this. Did I just take a risk?"

**Concerns.** Post-decision doubt. Did I get the scope right? Will my creative director
agree? What if the picture changes?

**Information they need.** Confirmation of exactly what was agreed (the signed text,
unchanged), what we need from them, and what the next seven days look like.

**Confidence to move.** "I know what happens next and who is doing it."

**Our job.** The countersignature writes the proposal from the *signed* band — never a
fresh quote (ADR-0067) — and the deal reaches Kickoff, the only surface that asks the
client for anything. A second way to close a deal is not done when the signature is
stored; the salesperson walks the client's path after it and makes sure the client's next
step is obvious and one click away.

**Where it lives.** Signature, countersignature, the proposal row, Kickoff.

### 1.8 Kickoff

**Thinking.** "Here's how we'll work together" — contacts, cadence, who reviews, how
many rounds, what a round means, how files arrive.

**Concerns.** Being asked for a deposit before they know the team. Being asked for the
cut before the edit is locked. Not knowing who to call.

**Information they need.** The team, the milestones, the communication plan, the revision
and delivery expectations, the escalation path — projected from what was agreed, not
typed again.

**Confidence to move.** "Everything is ready and nothing has reset." The workspace
principle: one link, nothing recreated.

**Our job.** Kickoff asks for the picture as well as the deposit, and is never *gated* on
the deposit. Every client action that can be taken carries the link that takes it. From
here the client lives in the room.

**Where it lives.** Kickoff readiness (`kickoff.readiness_for_project`), then the room.

### 1.9 Production

**Thinking.** "Is it going to be right? Where are we?"

**Concerns.** Silence. Not knowing whose court it is. A first take that misses the
direction. Being charged for a round they didn't know they'd spent. Their notes getting
lost between people.

**Information they need.** What is being made against which cut, whose turn it is, what
a note costs before it becomes work, how many rounds remain, when the next take lands.

**Confidence to move.** "I always know where it is, and nobody is going to surprise me
with a change order."

**Our job.** The room states the court by what it contains. A note is not work until it is
priced (ADR-0069): every client note is classified before a creator sees it, so a change
request arrives pre-priced and a round is spent knowingly. A new cut is parked until the
studio states the offset so every note moves with the picture. The salesperson's job in
production is to stay the single point of contact and to keep the commercial conversation
*out* of the creative one unless a note genuinely changes scope.

**Where it lives.** The room: brief, notes, takes, checklist.

### 1.10 Delivery

**Thinking.** "Is it actually done? Will legal accept this? Where are the files?"

**Concerns.** The download that unlocks before payment (fine) or the payment that is
demanded before they can check the files (not fine). A certificate that doesn't match the
final. Stems named three different ways. A cue sheet nobody filed.

**Information they need.** The delivery package: the final and its cutdowns, stems named
by an enforced convention, the clearance certificate bound to exactly what was delivered,
the cue sheet, the rights summary, the rollout matrix. And the invoice, with the same
numbers as the agreement.

**Confidence to move.** "I can hand this straight to business affairs and to the post
house and never think about it again."

**Our job.** Operational clarity *is* the deliverable. The final invoice rides the payment
seam from the portal; downloads unlock when it clears; the certificate follows the buyer
into the room. Nothing about delivery is improvised.

**Where it lives.** The delivery package, the certificate, the final invoice, the room.

### 1.11 Repeat client

**Thinking.** Next campaign: "Music — call Chordential." Ideally without a search.

**Concerns.** Will it be the same experience? Do they remember what we like? Will they
try to sell me more this time?

**Information they need.** That we remember: their revision habits, their approval chain,
their procurement path, the reference they disavowed, the renewal date on the last
licence. The next campaign should begin with accumulated knowledge, not a blank brief.

**Confidence to move.** "They already know how we work."

**Our job.** Campaign Intelligence becomes Relationship Intelligence. The renewal on the
previous licence is a real, dated, monetised event — the salesperson raises it *before*
it lapses, with the price that was agreed at signature. Repeat work is never a
re-qualification; it is a shorter discovery that starts from what we know.

### 1.12 Referral

**Thinking.** A peer says "who did the music on that?" or "my client's legal is asking
about AI provenance." The client thinks of us.

**Concerns.** Will they embarrass me? Will they treat my friend the way they treated me?
Will they try to use my name?

**Information they need.** None. But the *referred* person needs the same thing the
client needed at 1.2: one example, one glimpse of the paper.

**Confidence to move.** The confidence is entirely in their memory of delivery and of the
month after. Stage 8 is about earning it.

**Our job.** Make the referral effortless and safe. Record who introduced whom. Tell the
referrer their friend was looked after, without detail they didn't ask for.

## Stage 2 · The sales philosophy

### What kind of sales company Chordential is

**An advisory studio that qualifies harder than it pitches.** We are small on purpose,
senior on purpose, and clearance-first on purpose. We would rather have ten clients who
call us before they call anyone else than a hundred who took the cheapest quote once.

Specifically:

- **Diagnostic, not persuasive.** The first meeting is a diagnosis with a written
  result. A buyer who leaves it with a clear "not you, and here's who" has had a good
  meeting.
- **Fluent in procurement, led by craft.** We speak business affairs' language without
  apology, because the paper is where campaigns die. But we never lead a conversation
  with process; we lead with their work, and the paper is the *reason it's safe to say
  yes*.
- **One point of contact, all the way through.** The person who sold it is the person
  who answers during production. There is no hand-off to "the team." (The OS is what
  makes this possible for a small studio: the salesperson is never carrying the state in
  their head.)
- **Slow to close, fast to say no.** We do not chase. We do say early when it won't work.
- **Evidence-bound.** Every claim traces to something real: a delivered package with an
  invented brand, a certificate, a cue sheet, a signed agreement. When we have no real
  case study, we say we have none, and we offer the founding-client terms honestly
  (a launch rate in exchange for a named, approved story — paid, never free, one at a
  time).

### How we build trust

Trust is built by a small number of behaviours repeated without exception. They are
worth more than any sentence of copy.

1. **Say what we won't do, early.** No AI audio. No existing-song licensing. No
   catalogue. No pretending to be bigger than we are. A vendor who volunteers their edges
   is believed about their centre.
2. **Ask the questions the last vendor didn't.** Whether the budget includes the
   licence. Whether the delivery date is the air date. Who isn't on the call but can send
   it back. Which reference they are trying to *kill*. The questions are the expertise.
3. **Read it back, and send it the same day.** The written summary the client can
   correct is the single most trust-producing artefact we own. It proves we listened; it
   exposes what we got wrong while it is cheap; it becomes the proposal.
4. **Show the derivation.** The price is built, not quoted. Creative fee and licence fee
   are separate because they *are* separate; each input is named; anything assumed is
   labelled assumed. A number they can explain to someone else is a number they can sign.
5. **Never adjust the number on a surface.** One quote authority, rendered everywhere,
   adjusted nowhere (ADR-0034/0065). The Review and the proposal say the same deposit
   because they read the same quote.
6. **Keep the commercial and the creative apart.** The brief is "what are we making."
   The Review is "what are we agreeing to." Mixing them makes every creative note feel
   like a negotiation.
7. **Let the paperwork be real before the music is.** A sample licence and a sample
   certificate can be read by their business affairs before a note is written. That is
   the low-risk start a cautious buyer needs, and we offer it rather than a demo.
8. **Concede what is true.** We have no six-year track record with their clients. We
   cannot audit a composer's laptop. Saying so, and then saying what we *do* instead, is
   the moment buyers in the simulations visibly moved. Cleverness loses to candour.

### How we qualify

Qualification is the OS's job first and a human's decision second. The engine answers
whether the work is real, on-craft, clearable and budget-signalled; the Strategic-Value
lens answers whether the *buyer* compounds (a small door into a repeat agency outranks a
large one-off). The salesperson adds what only a conversation can:

| Question | Pass looks like | Fail looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Is this Chordential-shaped?** | Original composition, sonic identity, score, sound design, supervision of original work | Cover bands, DJs, playlists, karaoke, licensing existing songs, "just use the scratch" |
| **Is it real?** | A campaign, a date, a deliverable list, a person who can approve | "Exploring options," no date, no decision path |
| **Is it clearable by us?** | Work-for-hire or assigned, no uncleared samples, human authorship | Needs a famous song, needs a sample we can't clear, wants AI output warranted as original |
| **Can we deliver it *well*?** | Fits the approved roster and the calendar | Needs a live orchestra by Friday, or three flagship jobs at once |
| **Does the buyer compound?** | Agency, production company, brand team that runs campaigns | One-time event, a buyer type the CMO weights low |
| **Is the money real?** | An approved music number, all-in or clearly not, ceiling or target known | A campaign number mistaken for a music number; a "we'll see" |

The decision has four outcomes, and each is recorded with a reason:

- **Pursue** — the OS drafts, a human commits, the discovery call is booked.
- **Watch** — real but not now (picture not locked, budget not approved). It stays in
  the database and re-surfaces on the signal. Never nagged.
- **Refer** — real, not ours. We name who it is for (below).
- **Pass** — not real or not on-craft. Recorded, so the loop closes and the qualification
  model learns.

Capacity is a qualification criterion, not an afterthought. The COO's rule stands: sell
one, deliver it flawlessly, then open the next two. A blown first delivery poisons the
only proof we will have.

### When we say no

We say no — clearly, early, and with the reason — when:

- the work is not on-craft (see the table above);
- the client wants AI-generated audio, or wants us to certify something we did not make;
- the client wants an existing song licensed rather than original music made;
- the music budget is below the floor the quote authority reports, **and** the scope
  cannot honestly be reduced to meet it (we will always offer the reduced scope first);
- the timeline cannot be met without a quality we would not sign a certificate for;
- the calendar is full and taking the job would jeopardise a job already in production;
- the decision path is unknowable after discovery (no one can say who approves), because
  we will be building for a reviewer we have never heard from;
- the buyer's stated expectation of ownership, exclusivity or term cannot be priced in
  the band they have, and they have said the band is fixed;
- anything in the engagement would require us to imply a capability or a credential we
  do not have.

"No" is delivered in writing, in the same summary format as a "yes," with the reason and
the recommendation. It is never silence, never a slow fade, and never a quote so high it
does the saying-no for us.

### When we recommend another company

Recommending a competitor is a trust deposit that pays back for years, and it costs
nothing when the work was never ours. We recommend, by name where we can:

| The need | Send them to |
|---|---|
| Filler and internal cuts on a subscription budget | A production-music library — with the note about paid-media and cross-channel licence limits, so they come back when a piece is going to be *used* |
| Licensing a known song, or brokering a major-label sync | A music supervisor or a sync agency; we do not broker |
| A catalogue-scale programme (hundreds of tracks) | A library or a catalogue partner |
| Full live-orchestral recording at a scale beyond our roster | A larger music house or a scoring stage with their own contractor |
| Performers, bands, a jingle singer for an event | A talent agency |
| A vendor of record with a decade on a holding-company preferred list | Their incumbent — and we ask for the low-blast-radius job instead |
| A buyer who has decided AI-generated music is what they want | Nobody we recommend; we explain the clearance position once, plainly, and step back |

Every referral is recorded in the OS against the buyer, because the referral *is* the
relationship — the next time their problem is ours, we are the people who sent them
somewhere honest.

### Behaviours that create trust

- Asking a follow-up when the first answer was soft.
- Naming what was assumed on the surface that shows the number.
- Pausing before answering an objection, then naming the real concern before addressing
  it.
- Answering a hard question straight, then stopping talking.
- Keeping a call inside the time the client gave.
- Sending the summary the day of the call, and asking to be corrected.
- Putting a commitment in writing before the client has to ask for it.
- Telling a client their scope moved during the call, and that we caught it.
- Saying "I don't know, I'll find out" and then finding out.
- Raising a renewal before it lapses.
- Recommending someone else when someone else is right.

### Behaviours that destroy trust

- Quoting the client's budget back to them as the price.
- A trial-close with a haircut ("would it be crazy to pencil that in?"). Buyers name the
  technique out loud, and the salesperson has lost a point they cannot win back.
- A monologue. Sixty seconds of uninterrupted talk on a twenty-minute call is a cost the
  client pays in attention and remembers.
- Leading with process language before the work has been discussed.
- A second track, a credibility line and a P.S. stuffed into a first touch.
- "Let's stay in touch" as a close. Every conversation ends with one of three specific
  asks (a scoped call, a fixed written offer, or a deposit to start) or with a clear
  "not this one."
- Claiming a capability from the claims register before it is on the register.
- Any surface that shows a different number from another surface.
- Reaching a client's business-affairs contact before the producer has cleared us.
- "It got handled" as our own account of a problem. We say what happened.
- Silence during production.
- Asking for a referral in the delivery email.

## Stage 3 · The Discovery Call philosophy

This is not a script. The question bank lives in call prep and is regenerated for every
call; the rehearsal scripts and the answer-key tests live in `docs/call-scripts/` and
`docs/prep-sheet-test-call.md`. This section is the *thinking* those questions serve, so
that when a new hire is off the sheet — and they will be, because clients answer in the
wrong order — they still know what they are looking for.

### What a discovery call is for

Three outputs, in this order:

1. **Understanding** — enough to build a scope, a price and a schedule without guessing.
2. **A fit decision** — ours as much as theirs. We leave the call knowing whether to
   pursue, watch, refer or pass.
3. **A summary the client corrects** — sent the same day, read back at the end of the
   call, with every open question marked open.

If the call produces a warm feeling and no summary, it was a chat.

### What we must understand

Seven territories. The sheet covers them; the salesperson holds them as a map, so a gap
is visible even when the conversation wanders.

| Territory | What we must leave knowing | The follow-up that earns the fee |
|---|---|---|
| **The work** | What the campaign is trying to do for the business; how they'll know it worked | The success measure (trial, donations in ten days, "comments about the film not the price") is what the music has to serve |
| **The music's job** | Carrying or supporting; every version needed; stems, and on which; formats | The unnamed placement — the in-store loop, the fundraising-dinner screening, the fourth vertical picked up last week |
| **The sound** | The emotional arc; whether it turns or holds; the references *and the disavowed ones* | Which *part* of a reference they're reacting to; which reference they are trying to kill |
| **The plan** | Air date **and** delivery date, separately; what upstream is still moving; when socials land | The picture lock, the colour grade, the shoot that keeps getting rained off |
| **The money** | The approved *music* number; all-in with licence or not; ceiling or target | The distinction between the campaign figure and the music line |
| **The people** | Who approves day-to-day; who can send it back; how many times they'll see it; who moves paper and how fast | The second name — the founder who sees it once at the end, the brand director who isn't a music person |
| **The paper** | Media, territory, term (from when), renewal, exclusivity, publishing, PRO/cue sheets, payment terms and deposit, musicians' requirements | Whether an extension is committed or a possibility; whether the price of renewal should be fixed now |

### What we never assume

Each of these has cost a deal, a schedule or a margin somewhere. The prep-sheet tests
were written around them.

- **That the number they gave is the music number.** A campaign budget is not a music
  budget; the ratio can be three to one.
- **That the air date is the delivery date.** There is always a stretch between them,
  and something (station clearance, a media agency, a board meeting) lives in it.
- **That the person on the call approves.** "Me, mostly" is followed by a founder, a
  board, a brand director.
- **That a reference is something to chase.** Half the references a client sends are
  what they are trying to avoid.
- **That an extension is a territory.** "Maybe the UK in spring" is a possibility to
  note, never a right to price.
- **That silence is agreement.** A question asked and not answered (publishing, most
  often) is an *open question* in the summary, never a captured fact.
- **That exclusivity is free.** It is the term clients most often assume costs nothing
  and the one the licence prices most steeply.
- **That the client knows the vocabulary.** Stems, cue sheets, PRO registration,
  publishing: ask whether they'd like it explained, and explain it only when the answer
  changes what we do.
- **That scope is fixed once said.** It moves mid-call. The read-back is where it is
  caught.
- **That a soft number is a number.** "Around sixty" is not a figure until we know
  whether it is all-in and whether it is a ceiling.
- **That we are the right partner.** The call exists to find out.

### How we uncover hidden requirements

Hidden requirements are not hidden by the client. They are unasked. The mechanism is the
**follow-up**: almost every first answer has a second half that only appears if the next
question is asked. The prep-sheet exercise proves it: six of the things that decide a deal
are reachable *only* through a follow-up.

Three questions reach the things no sheet can list:

- **"What haven't I asked about that I should have?"** It surfaces the footage that
  doesn't exist yet, the stems by group versus by instrument, the choir of children.
- **"Has music let you down before?"** Every previous failure is a requirement in
  disguise: the track that turned up in a car ad (exclusivity); the piece forty seconds
  too short to cut down (structure and stems); the free track nobody wanted to reject
  (a candid approval path).
- **"Who isn't here who has an opinion?"** The absent reviewer is the most common cause
  of a late direction change.

And one discipline: **the read-back.** Playing the whole picture back at the end, and
asking to be stopped where it's wrong, is the cheapest moment to catch a wrong number and
the line most often skipped when a call runs long. It is not skipped.

### How we identify risk

Risk is asked for directly, because clients rarely volunteer it and always know it.

| Risk | The tell | What we do with it |
|---|---|---|
| **Schedule** | Anything upstream still moving; a delivery date inside a board or finance cycle | It goes in the summary as a dependency with an owner; the timeline is built from the delivery date, not the air date |
| **Approval** | A second approver who sees it once, late, and isn't a music person | We plan a checkpoint for them earlier than "the end," and we make the work legible to a non-musician |
| **Scope** | Numbers that changed during the call; an "oh, and one more thing" | The summary carries the *last* number and names that it moved |
| **Rights** | Uncommitted territories; exclusivity "non-negotiable after what happened"; publishing unknown | Priced as stated, with the extension shown as an option and the open item marked open |
| **Payment** | No deposit possible; finance in on two days a week; two signatures over a threshold | The Commercial Review adapts the schedule; we don't discover it at invoice time |
| **Reference** | A reference sent by someone senior that the producer privately dislikes | The disavowed reference is recorded as what to avoid, and the direction is built toward what the decision-maker actually likes |
| **Ours** | The job needs something the roster or calendar can't give | We say so on the call, and pursue, watch, refer or pass honestly |

### How we discover budget without making it awkward

Budget is awkward when it is asked *first* or asked *bare*. It stops being awkward when
it is the natural end of a conversation about what the music has to do, and when the
follow-up shows we asked for a reason.

The sequence, as thinking rather than lines:

1. **Work before money.** Understand the job, the versions, the usage. By the time money
   comes up, both sides know what is being priced.
2. **Ask for the approved music number, not "the budget."** The specific noun does the
   work: it separates the music line from the campaign figure without anyone feeling
   caught out.
3. **Always ask whether it includes the licence, and whether it is a ceiling or a
   target.** This is the one follow-up that cannot be skipped. It is also the moment the
   client realises we price usage as a separate thing, which sets up the proposal.
4. **Do not react to the number.** It is a check, not a price. The quote authority will
   return a verdict (below floor, in band, above band); the salesperson doesn't quote
   the number back, doesn't flinch, doesn't celebrate.
5. **If it's not going to work, say so before the call ends.** A band well below the
   floor with a fixed scope is a "not this one" delivered kindly and in writing, not a
   proposal designed to lose.
6. **Never treat the budget as the price.** ADR-0065 is binding: what the client said
   their budget is never sets the price. The fee is cost at margin plus licence. If the
   two happen to agree, good; if they don't, the itemised derivation is how we say so.

### How we establish expertise without dominating

Expertise at Chordential shows in three places, none of which is a speech.

- **In the questions.** A client who is asked whether the term runs from delivery or
  from first air has learned something about us, and the call is still theirs.
- **In one teach moment.** One thing they didn't know, chosen for *this* buyer, said in
  under a minute, and then back to questions. The AI-provenance warranty appearing in
  client MSAs; the paid-media lift that kills a library licence; the renewal that is
  cheaper agreed now. One. Not three.
- **In the summary.** The written document — what they said, what we assumed, what is
  open, what we recommend — is where the expertise lives, and it arrives after they've
  hung up. It cannot dominate a conversation; it can only be read.

Rules of conduct on the call: the client talks more than we do; a term is explained only
when asked or when the answer depends on it; a hard question gets a straight answer and
then silence; the time they gave is the time we take.

### After the call

The summary goes out the same day, to the people the client named — not to the ones they
said "not yet" about. Every open question is marked open. The notes the OS captured are
checked against what the salesperson heard: a value in the wrong slot is corrected before
the client ever sees it. Corrections from the client are welcomed and applied, because the
summary is about to become the proposal and the thing the signature covers.

## Stage 4 · The proposal philosophy

At Chordential the Discovery Summary *is* the proposal (ADR-0065). There is no separate
document that restates it. What the client read and corrected after the call grows a
scope, a fee, terms and an Agreement block, and the text they sign is the text they read.
This section is about what that document has to accomplish and how it presents money.

### What every proposal accomplishes

1. **Proves we heard.** The scope reads as the client's own words organised, not our
   template filled in. The disavowed reference is there. The fourth vertical is there. The
   date that moved is the moved date.
2. **Names what we assumed.** Every inferred input a figure rests on is labelled stated
   or assumed (`estimation.Scope` pairs each value with whether the brief said it). A
   guess presented as a fact is the honesty rule broken, not a rounding error.
3. **Prices what was asked for, in two parts, with the derivation shown.** Creative fee
   and licence fee, each built from named inputs, with the budget verdict alongside.
4. **Makes the decision easy to explain to someone who wasn't on the call.** The head of
   production, the founder, the board. If it needs the salesperson in the room to make
   sense, it isn't finished.
5. **Says what happens next and what it needs from them.** The approval, the deposit,
   the picture, the names.
6. **Makes "no" easy.** A clear scope with a clear price is easy to decline, and an easy
   decline is a client who comes back for the next job.

### Questions it answers before the client asks

- What exactly are we making, and how many versions, in which formats?
- Where will it run, for how long, from when, in which territories, exclusive to whom?
- What happens when the term ends, and what does renewal cost — decided now?
- Who owns what? Where does publishing sit?
- How many rounds are included, what counts as a round, and what does another cost?
- What happens if the picture changes after we start?
- What will you need from us, and by when? (Picture, references, names.)
- Who is our single point of contact, and how do we reach them?
- What do we get on delivery — certificate, cue sheet, stems, package — and what do they
  look like? (Sample deliverables with invented brands, labelled.)
- How and when do we pay, and does it fit our finance cycle?
- What did you assume, because we didn't tell you?
- What are you *not* doing? (No AI audio. No existing-song licensing. The reduced scope
  we offered instead of the one below floor.)

### What creates confidence

- The client's own numbers and names, correct.
- Two fees, itemised, so the "why not one number like the other house" question is
  answered on the page.
- The budget verdict shown honestly: in band, or not, and what changes if not.
- Options that are *scopes*, each protecting against a named risk from discovery.
- A renewal price fixed now.
- Terms in a producer's voice, short, specific to this job.
- The same deposit on the Review as on the proposal, because they read one quote.
- A sample of the paper — certificate, cue sheet — so business affairs can read the
  format before anyone commits.
- A signature that is a real electronic signature bound to the exact text (SHA-256 of
  the signable text; a drawn mark optional), and that reports itself superseded if the
  text ever changes.

### What creates hesitation

- A number with no derivation.
- A number that equals the budget. (It reads as "they charged what we had.")
- Scope that is broader or narrower than what was said, silently.
- Boilerplate. A terms block that could belong to any job belongs to no job.
- Rendered HTML as the thing signed, or acceptance copy that lives in a template
  (forbidden — it is part of the signed document).
- A surface that disagrees with another surface.
- An option that exists to make the recommended option look cheap.
- Pressure of any kind: a deadline we invented, a "founding rate" that is really a
  countdown.
- Anything the client has to ask the salesperson to explain.

### How Chordential presents pricing

- **Two fees, always.** A creative fee — cost at target margin, from the estimator, not
  second-guessed — and a licence fee — media, territory, term, exclusivity, capped. They
  are separate because they are separate: one is what it costs to make, the other is what
  the use is worth. A buyer who wants one number gets one total *and* the two parts.
- **The budget is only a check.** The verdict (below floor / in band / above band) is
  shown; the price is never moved to meet the budget. If the verdict is below floor, the
  proposal shows the reduced scope that fits, or it is a "not this one."
- **The derivation is on the page.** Every factor that moved the licence — the
  exclusivity, the perpetual term, the all-media reach — is a named line. When a deal hits
  the licence cap it is reported as a negotiating position, not hidden as arithmetic.
- **Assumed inputs are named next to the number.** Rounds assumed, duration assumed,
  territory assumed.
- **No surface adjusts the quote.** The proposal, the Review, the workspace and the room
  render `quote_for`; none of them re-converts, discounts or rounds it.
- **The pricing model has one page** (`/pricing`), which reads the factor tables and
  prices a worked example through the real quote builder. A salesperson who wants to
  understand *why* a licence costs what it costs reads that page and the market research
  behind it (`docs/market-pricing-research.md`). Neither is quoted to a client as a rate
  card; the proposal is the only place a client sees a number.
- **The founding-client rate**, while it exists, is described exactly as what it is: a
  launch rate for early clients who let us tell their story, paid, one at a time, with a
  case study approved in writing. Never a limited-time device.

### How optional services are introduced

Options are **scopes that protect against a named risk**, discovered on the call. They are
not upsells, and they are never presented as a menu.

- **Each option answers a "what if" the client raised.** The UK extension they mentioned.
  The renewal they'd rather fix now. The cinema placement at Christmas that isn't booked.
  The choir they wondered about. Stems by instrument rather than by group.
- **The default is the one we recommend**, and we say why. A proposal with three equal
  options is a proposal that asks the client to do our job.
- **Three at most.** More is a catalogue.
- **An option is priced through the same quote authority** and carries its own stated /
  assumed labels. No option is a round number chosen to look attractive.
- **Nothing is pre-selected that costs money.** Kickoff asks for the deposit and the
  picture; it never asks the client to decline an add-on.
- **An option declined is recorded**, so the next campaign for this client starts knowing
  what they didn't want last time — and so the renewal or the extension can be raised
  when it becomes real.

### The Commercial Review

The Review is the commercial conversation, held separately from the creative one:
scope, deliverables, timeline, pricing, deposit, payment schedule, producer-voiced terms,
a procurement checklist, and one act of approval. It is projected from Campaign
Intelligence and the quote, frozen at operator release, and approved electronically in
the workspace. That approval is the award trigger; it creates the project and moves the
deal to Kickoff.

The salesperson's job at the Review is procurement fluency: discover how this organisation
buys (their paper or ours, PO or not, vendor portal, W-9, certificate of insurance,
payment cycle, signature thresholds) and make sure the checklist names each of them with
an owner. We adapt to their procurement; we do not integrate into it.

## Stage 5 · The objection framework

### The method, before the list

Every objection is handled the same way, whatever it is:

1. **Pause.** A beat before answering signals the question was worth thinking about.
2. **Name the real concern**, in a sentence, and check it. ("Sounds like the risk you
   are pricing isn't the music, it's the unknowns of a new vendor on a client that
   matters. Is that right?") Getting this wrong is fine; the client corrects it, and now
   we are talking about the real thing.
3. **Ask one clarifying question**, usually about the last time it went wrong.
4. **Answer with mechanics, not adjectives.** What is in the agreement, what the room
   does, what the certificate says.
5. **Concede what is true.** Then stop talking.

An objection *landed* is not an objection *won*. The simulations show most objections
land partially; the client keeps a reservation and moves anyway because the honesty was
worth more than the answer. That is the standard.

### The objections

**Budget: "Too expensive."**
- *Real concern:* Can I justify this internally against a library subscription or a
  freelancer's per-minute rate?
- *How we think:* Those comparators price one person's writing time. They do not price a
  producer, a mix, clearance, cue sheets, stems, delivery, or the guarantee that any of it
  arrives. Comparing us to them is comparing a studio to a session player — and it is
  the comparison a price-shopping buyer will make, so we answer it without defensiveness:
  the music is the part you can buy cheaper; the certainty is what the fee is.
- *Evidence:* The itemised derivation. The clearance certificate. The last time a
  library track needed a licence upgrade after a paid-media lift. The reduced scope we
  can offer if the band is real.

**Budget: "The other house quotes one number."**
- *Real concern:* Your two fees look bigger than their one, and I have to explain why.
- *How we think:* The other house's number has a licence inside it too; it just isn't
  shown. Ours is shown so it can be negotiated (term, territory, exclusivity are levers
  the client controls) and so renewal is a known number rather than a surprise.
- *Evidence:* The licence lines on the proposal; the renewal price fixed now.

**Budget: "You're in a weird middle."**
- *Real concern:* Are you cheap-and-risky or expensive-and-safe? I don't know which
  drawer to put you in.
- *How we think:* The middle is deliberate for a first job: cheap enough that failure is
  survivable, real enough that success means something. We say that.
- *Evidence:* A low-blast-radius first job, judged on the whole delivery.

**Timeline: "We need it in a week." / "The edit isn't locked."**
- *Real concern:* Will you be late, and will you charge me when my side is the reason?
- *How we think:* The timeline is built from the delivery date, not the air date, with
  upstream dependencies named and owned. A cut that arrives is parked until the offset is
  stated so notes move with the picture. If the calendar genuinely cannot hold the
  quality we sign for, we say so before the deposit, not after.
- *Evidence:* The milestone plan in Kickoff; the dependency list in the summary; the
  room's court state.

**Internal composer / incumbent music house: "We have people for this."**
- *Real concern:* Why would I risk a relationship that answers the phone on Friday night?
- *How we think:* They shouldn't, on a flagship. We ask for the job the incumbents are too
  busy or too expensive for, and we ask to be judged on the whole delivery — music, stems,
  cutdowns and the rights file — against what the incumbent delivers on a comparable job.
  We do not disparage the incumbent. We often recommend keeping them.
- *Evidence:* The delivery package, the certificate and the cue sheet, side by side with
  theirs.

**AI: "Why not just use Suno?" / "Your no-AI guarantee is unenforceable."**
- *Real concern (first form):* AI is free; what am I paying for?
- *Real concern (second form):* A warranty is a piece of paper; my client holds the
  lawsuit.
- *How we think:* AI-only output has no clean chain of title, cannot be cleanly licensed
  or indemnified, and the warranty language is arriving in client MSAs whether or not
  anyone is ready for it. We do not generate audio, we say so in the agreement, and every
  creator signs a standing agreement that says so too. And we concede the true thing: no
  one can watch a composer's laptop, including the incumbents. The difference is a studio
  built for the question versus one hoping nobody asks it.
- *Evidence:* The clearance certificate's chain-of-title language; the composer and
  service agreements; the no-AI position in copy. **Nothing beyond the claims register:**
  no insurance, no archive, no audit is claimed until the operator has verified and
  listed it.

**Rights: "Who owns it?" / "What about publishing?"**
- *Real concern:* Will this come back to bite my client in two years?
- *How we think:* There is one ratified default and a salesperson states it the same way
  every time (ADR-0032): **the client buys the recording outright and holds a perpetual
  sync licence across every campaign medium, worldwide, exclusive in their category; the
  composition's publishing stays with Chordential Music**, which is what the cue sheet
  files and what performance royalties are collected against. In the client's words: *you
  own the recording and the right to use it forever, in every campaign medium, and there
  is nothing to clear but ours.* Per-deal overrides are possible and are priced. The words
  "full buyout" and "work-made-for-hire" are never used, because they describe a different
  structure than the one we sign and a business-affairs reviewer catches the contradiction
  on one read. When a client does not know their own position, it goes in the summary as
  an open question for their legal to push back on — never as a captured fact.
- *Evidence:* The rights summary in the delivery package; the signed text; the
  certificate bound to exactly what was delivered.

**Licensing: "Our library subscription covers this." / "Forever, everywhere, please."**
- *Real concern (first):* Why pay for a licence when I have one?
- *Real concern (second):* I don't want to come back and pay again every year.
- *How we think:* Platform and library licences typically cover native use; the paid
  lift, the cross-channel repost and next year's re-use are where they fail, and it is
  usually the producer who has to go back and upgrade. Perpetual, worldwide, exclusive
  is a real thing we can price; it is also the most expensive thing on the sheet, and
  most clients, shown the derivation, choose a term with a renewal price fixed now.
- *Evidence:* The licence lines; the cap reported as a negotiating position; the
  renewal date recorded and raised before it lapses.

**Procurement: "Our paper, our MSA, a PO first, six weeks of onboarding."**
- *Real concern:* Will you fight our process, and will it delay the job?
- *How we think:* We don't fight it, and we don't integrate with it. The rule is
  ADR-0022: **ChordOS prepares clients for procurement, it never integrates with
  procurement systems.** Requirements are discovered from what this client actually said
  — two clients get two different checklists — and the documents we can legally produce
  (W-9, ACH authorisation, remittance, vendor profile, company overview) are generated
  from one company profile. What we cannot legally auto-produce (a certificate of
  insurance, an MSA, an NDA, a security questionnaire) comes back as a request for the
  missing piece, never as a fake official form. A vendor portal gets a guided upload, not
  a connector. A deposit that cannot be paid because a grant pays on delivery is a
  payment schedule to design, not a deal-breaker.
- *Evidence:* The procurement checklist in the Review with owners and dates; the
  electronic approval record (name, email, timestamp, approved text).

**New vendor risk: "Your downside is unbounded."**
- *Real concern:* I know which mistakes my incumbents make. I don't know yours.
- *How we think:* Agree. Never ask to start on the flagship. Offer the paper first — a
  sample licence, a sample certificate, the agreement format — so business affairs can
  read us before a note is written. Offer a low-stakes job. Put every commitment in
  writing before they ask.
- *Evidence:* The sample deliverables; the written commitments; a reference call from a
  real client once one exists, and an honest "we have none yet" until then.

**Small company concerns: "What's your Friday-night story?" / "What if you get hit by a
bus?"**
- *Real concern:* Will you be there when it goes wrong, and does everything live in one
  head?
- *How we think:* We are small; we say so. What we offer instead of scar tissue is
  *written* commitments (response expectations in the agreement, a named single point of
  contact, an escalation path in Kickoff) and an operating system in which nothing lives
  in one head: every note, version, approval and rights fact is in the room, and the
  client's own link never changes.
- *Evidence:* The room; the Kickoff communication plan; the agreement. **Response-time
  commitments are made only as written in the agreement, never as a spoken number.**

**Revisions: "What happens when the client hates V1?"**
- *Real concern:* This is where music vendors die — extra demo fees and a composer's ego
  on the call.
- *How we think:* Rounds are named in the agreement; a round is a version, and a note
  is priced before it becomes work, in the room, where both sides see it. A change
  request arrives pre-priced. The composer is contracted to us, so managing them is our
  job, not the client's. And the honest caveat: a sixth round because someone's spouse
  has opinions is a change conversation like anywhere.
- *Evidence:* The room's note classification (conform / revision / out of scope); the
  round ledger; the revision terms in the signed text.

**"Procurement-grade is jargon. Nobody wins a pitch with paperwork."**
- *Real concern:* Are you a music studio or a compliance vendor?
- *How we think:* They are right that nobody buys the paperwork. We ask about the last
  time documentation held up a payment or an air date. Then we talk about the work.
- *Evidence:* Craft first: one example that sounds like their kind of job. The paper
  second, as the reason it is safe to say yes.

**"Send me some information."**
- *Real concern:* I want to end this conversation politely.
- *How we think:* We send one page and name the specific next step it leads to, or we
  agree that this is a "not now" and record it as a watch. Never a brochure, never a
  drip.
- *Evidence:* The first-touch page; the capabilities document once there is interest.

**"Can we hear something first?" (spec work)**
- *Real concern:* I want to de-risk the creative before I commit.
- *How we think:* We do not write on spec; it devalues the craft we exist to serve and
  it is how a studio ends up building toward a reference the client is trying to kill.
  What we offer instead is a scoped, paid first step (a short-form job, or the founding
  engagement) and a direction conversation in the room before the first take. We show
  demonstrations with invented brands, labelled as such.
- *Evidence:* The sample deliverables; the room's brief layer, where direction is agreed
  before composition.

**"We'll go with the cheapest quote."**
- *Real concern:* Price is the only variable I am allowed to defend.
- *How we think:* Then we may not be the right partner for this job, and we say so
  kindly, with the referral. We record the loss and the reason. We check in once, later,
  and leave them alone.
- *Evidence:* None needed. The behaviour is the evidence, next time.

## Stage 6 · Sales assets

Assets are evidence, not persuasion. Each exists to answer one question at one moment.
Introducing an asset before its moment is the most common way a small studio looks like a
marketing department.

| Asset | Answers | Exists today? | Introduced at | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Front-of-house site** (`/`, `/score`) | "Do these people care about craft?" | Yes | Never heard / Aware | Demonstrations use invented brands and say so. No claims that cannot be shown. |

| **Discovery Summary** | "Did they hear me?" | Yes | Same day as the call | The proposal grows from it. Open questions marked open. |
| **Sample deliverables** (certificate, cue sheet, delivery package, room walkthrough) | "What will business affairs receive?" | Yes (demo brands) | Discovery → Proposal, or earlier for a paper-first buyer | Labelled as demonstrations. The paper is offered *before* the music to a cautious buyer. |
| **Pricing guide** (how pricing is built) | "Why two fees, and what moves the licence?" | Partly — the internal model page exists; a client-facing explanation of the *structure* is owed by the Content Strategy | Proposal | Never a rate card. Explains the levers; the proposal carries the numbers. |

| **Reference call** | "Can I ask someone who has worked with you?" | After the first delivered client agrees | New-vendor objection | Offered, not volunteered. The client's time is asked for sparingly. |
| **Client portal** (workspace → room) | "Where do I go, and does the link ever change?" | Yes | Shown in discovery; lived in from Commitment | One link. Nothing resets. Shown as the answer to "how do we work together," not as a feature tour. |
| **Video walkthrough** (the room, the delivery package) | "What does production feel like?" | Not yet — owed by the Content Strategy | Proposal → Kickoff, and for the second approver who never sat on a call | Made with demo brands. Under three minutes. Shows the court state, a priced note, the certificate. |
| **The written "no"** | "Why not, and who instead?" | Yes (the summary format) | Whenever the answer is no | Same care as a yes. |

Three standing rules:

- **One piece of proof per moment.** A track *or* a certificate *or* a page. A wall of
  assets reads as a catalogue.
- **The asset the buyer needs is decided by their objection, not by our sequence.** A
  business-affairs buyer gets the paper first. A creative director gets the example
  first.
- **Every asset traces to the claims register and the honesty rule.** An asset that
  implies a real client we do not have is withdrawn, whoever made it.

## Stage 7 · The client experience

The feeling at each moment, what produces it, and what breaks it. A salesperson is
responsible for the feeling even where the surface is the OS's.

| Moment | Should feel | Produced by | Broken by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Before discovery** | Curious, unpressured. "This won't cost me anything but twenty minutes." | A specific, short, human first touch; a scoped call with a defined output; nothing asked for | A brochure; a follow-up cadence; a link they have to click to understand the email |
| **After discovery** | Understood, and slightly surprised. "They asked things nobody asks." | The follow-ups; the one teach moment; the read-back; the summary in their inbox the same day with the open questions honest | A summary that says $90k where they said $6k; a summary two days late; a summary copied to someone they said "not yet" about |
| **After proposal** | Clear, and able to explain it to someone else. "Nothing hidden." | Two fees itemised; assumptions named; options tied to their what-ifs; a renewal price now; terms in a producer's voice | A number equal to the budget; boilerplate; a surface that disagrees with another; anything that needs the salesperson to decode |
| **At commitment** | Safe. "I know what happens next." | The signed text unchanged; Kickoff asking for the picture and the deposit with the links that do it; one point of contact named | A fresh quote after signature; a deposit demanded before the team is known; a second link that goes somewhere else |
| **During production** | Informed, never chased and never chasing. "I always know whose court it is." | The room stating the court; a note priced before it is work; takes arriving when promised; silence only when there is nothing to say | Radio silence; a surprise change order; a note lost between people; a cut that moved with nothing moving with it |
| **During review** | In control of the creative, without a negotiation. "Leaving a note is safe." | Timecoded notes that are theirs to move and edit; the second verdict phrased as asking for a new version, not "request changes"; a pending take never shown as approved | A note that triggers a fee conversation with no warning; a reviewer link that answers with a login page; an unvetted take presented as done |
| **During delivery** | Relief, then a quiet respect. "I can hand this straight to legal and the post house." | The package: certificate bound to the final, cue sheet, stems by convention, rights summary; the final invoice with the agreed number; downloads unlocked the moment it clears | A certificate that doesn't match the final; a payment demanded before they can check the files; stems named three ways |
| **One month later** | Remembered. "They checked in about the thing I actually needed." | A single check-in about the campaign's result and the renewal date; a credit that appeared where it should; nothing sold | A survey; a referral ask; a newsletter |
| **One year later** | Loyal without effort. "Music — call Chordential." | The renewal raised before it lapsed, at the price agreed; the next brief starting from what we already know; the same person answering | Re-qualification; a blank brief; a new point of contact; a renewal they discovered by the licence lapsing |

The emotional arc across the whole journey is the same one the Experience bible gives the
website: *silence → first note → swell → full arrangement → resolution → invitation.*
Discovery is the first note. Delivery is the resolution. Referral is the invitation — and
it is the client's to extend, not ours to ask for.

## Stage 8 · The referral system

### The principle

**Referrals are earned at delivery and in the month after it, and they are never asked
for in the delivery email.** A delighted client becomes an advocate the way anyone does:
by being asked, by a peer, who did the music, and by having a story worth telling. Our
job is to make sure the story is good, that the artefacts of it travel, and that the
friend who arrives is looked after.

A referral strategy that feels *requested* turns a trusted relationship transactional at
the precise moment it was becoming durable. So: no bounties paid to clients, no "know
anyone who…" in a follow-up, no referral link. Those belong to a different kind of
company.

### How delighted clients naturally become advocates

1. **The delivery is the story.** A producer who hands business affairs a certificate,
   a cue sheet and a rights summary and never hears about it again has a sentence ready
   for the next peer who asks about music: "they made it not my problem." That sentence
   is the referral. Everything in Stages 1–7 exists to earn it.
2. **The artefacts travel without us.** The certificate goes to legal. The delivery
   package goes to the post house. The room link goes to the creative director who never
   sat on a call. Each of those people meets Chordential through the paperwork before
   they meet a salesperson, and each is a future buyer or a future referrer. The
   procurement-grade rule — every artefact reads as though it came from a company ten
   times our size — is a referral strategy.
3. **The composer's credit is honest and present.** A creator who is chosen, respected
   and paid promptly tells other creators, and creators tell producers. The supply side
   refers the demand side more often than any pipeline shows.
4. **We refer first.** Every honest recommendation of another company (Stage 2) is a
   referral we gave. Studios and supervisors who received one send back the work that is
   ours. The OS records both directions.
5. **The one-month check-in is about them.** Did the campaign do what it was supposed
   to? Did the trial number, the ten-day donations, the comments land? Is the renewal
   date in their diary? Nothing is sold. A client who is asked how their campaign went,
   by the person who did the music, remembers it — and mentions it.
6. **The case study is offered, never extracted.** When a client is plainly proud of the
   work, we offer to tell the story with their approval, in writing, and with the
   founding-client terms already honoured if they applied. The story is theirs; we ask
   permission for every word, and "no" changes nothing about the relationship.
7. **The renewal is a referral moment.** Raising a renewal before it lapses, at the
   price agreed a year ago, is the most surprising thing a music vendor can do. It is also
   when a client is most likely to say "actually, we have another campaign — and my
   counterpart at X was asking."

### What the referred person experiences

- **They are told who sent them, if the referrer allowed it, and nothing else about the
  referrer's work.** Confidentiality of the previous engagement is absolute.
- **They get the same discovery as everyone.** A referral is not a shortcut through
  qualification; it is a warm start to it. If we are not the right partner for the
  friend's job, we say so — and the referrer hears that their friend was treated
  honestly, which is worth more than a sale.
- **They are never worse off for having been referred.** No inflated expectation set by
  the referrer goes unaddressed; no "your friend got X" pricing exists to be compared.

### What the referrer experiences

- **A thank-you, once, in a sentence.** Not a gift, not a fee, not a discount.
- **Confirmation that the friend was looked after**, without detail.
- **No change to their own engagement.** The referral buys nothing and costs nothing.
- **Their name in our memory.** The OS records the introduction against both buyers, so
  a year later the relationship intelligence knows this client is a connector, and the
  salesperson knows to keep their check-ins genuinely about them.

### What the OS holds

Every introduction is recorded: who referred whom, when, and what came of it (won, lost,
referred elsewhere, watched). Referral-sourced deals are tagged so that win/loss capture
can say, honestly, what proportion of work came from clients rather than from outbound —
the number the Growth Strategy needs and the only advocacy metric this playbook allows.
Volume of referrals is never a target; a referral that was asked for is not counted.

## Appendix A · How a new salesperson uses this playbook

**Week one.** Read the Constitution, then this document, then the Discovery Guide and the
two rehearsal scripts. Run the prep-sheet test call against a colleague playing the
client from `docs/prep-sheet-test-call.md` or `docs/roleplay-larkspur-trust.md`. Score it
the way the document scores it: which follow-ups did you not ask? Read a delivery package
with an invented brand end to end, including the certificate and the cue sheet. Read the
claims register.

**Week two.** Sit in on a real discovery call with the notetaker running. Write the
summary yourself before reading the OS's; compare. Read the Commercial Review for a live
deal and find every place a procurement checklist item has an owner. Walk the client's
path from countersignature to the room and note anything that would confuse a client.

**Week three onward.** Own conversations. Every one ends with one of three asks or a
clear "not this one." Every pursued opportunity gets an outcome and a reason in the OS.
Every proposal names what it assumed. Every delivered job gets a one-month check-in that
sells nothing.

**The weekly motion** (from the Revenue Drive council, unchanged): a named list, real
conversations rather than blasts, one founding client sold and delivered before the next
two are opened, the funnel tracked on `/revenue` — conversations → qualified → summaries
sent → proposals signed → deposits → delivered → paid in full → repeat → referred.

## Appendix B · What we measure

Volume is never a success metric (Constitution §4.7). The numbers a salesperson is held
to:

- Qualified conversations, and the pursue / watch / refer / pass split with reasons.
- Summaries sent the same day, and the proportion the client corrected (a correction is
  a success: it means they read it and it was cheap to fix).
- Proposals signed, and the time from summary to signature.
- Deposits collected; deliveries on the agreed date; final invoices paid without a
  dispute about scope.
- Repeat campaigns per client; renewals raised before lapse.
- Referral-sourced deals, unasked.
- Win/loss reasons captured on every pursued opportunity, without exception.

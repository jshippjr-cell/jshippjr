The Discovery Call Playbook

How to run a Chordential discovery call — grounded in how ad agencies actually buy music, and in the strongest available evidence on what makes B2B discovery calls convert. For every Chordential seller, on every call.

What we sell — say it the same way every time

Clearance-certified original music, made by people. One signature covers every right — master and publishing, worldwide, in perpetuity. Delivered procurement-ready: stems, cutdowns, and complete rights documentation. Guaranteed no AI-generated audio. Engagements typically run $4,000–$90,000.

How to use this playbook

  • Before a call: read pages 2–3 (who you're talking to, the numbers to know cold) and run the prep checklist (p. 4).
  • During: the call structure and question arc (pp. 5–6) and the objection table (p. 7) are the working pages.
  • After: the follow-through routine (p. 8). Your call is recorded and transcribed into Campaign Intelligence automatically — your job on the call is to listen, not take notes.
  • The one-page cheat sheet (p. 9) is printable on its own.

The five principles behind everything here

1
Sell like an expert, not a persuader. Buyers of professional services pick on reputation and specialized expertise; cost is the deciding factor only ~8% of the time — sellers overestimate price sensitivity almost 2-to-1 (Hinge Research, 1,000+ buyer studies). Your calm command of the music-clearance domain IS the pitch.
2
Questions do the selling. The largest observational study of selling ever run (Rackham/Huthwaite, ~35,000 calls) found that in considered purchases — exactly our deal size — top performers win by asking questions that surface a problem's consequences, not by pitching or closing hard.
3
Teach one thing. Independent research on 700+ B2B purchases (RAIN Group) found winners educate buyers with a new perspective ~3× more often than runners-up. Every call should leave the buyer knowing something they didn't (see "teach moments," p. 6).
4
Disqualify early and honestly. A small team can't afford long unqualified pursuits. If there's no music need, no budget authority in reach, or a done deal with an incumbent, say so warmly and leave the door open. (Sandler's core discipline — the part of it that fits us.)
5
The machine remembers; you connect. Every call is recorded, transcribed, and extracted into Campaign Intelligence. Confirm the record after — don't split attention during.

The two structural facts that shape every deal

1. Music usually happens in post. In most campaigns the spot is shot first; music is sourced against a finished cut, under compressed timelines — supervisors report clearance windows as short as 24–48 hours in social-first work. Unilever's marketing VP said it publicly: music is "too often an afterthought." Your fast, structured delivery is built for exactly this moment — and your long game is getting briefed at script stage instead.

2. Producers buy from a habitual shortlist. A handful of trusted sources — often three to five — supply most searches. The goal of your first deal is not the fee; it's a place on the shortlist. Price the first engagement to win it.

Part two · The call

Before the call

Speed is a strategy

When a discovery request comes in, respond within the hour — ideally within minutes. The classic lead-response study (MIT/InsideSales) found contact odds ~100× higher at 5 minutes vs 30; HBR's audit found firms responding within an hour were ~7× likelier to qualify the lead, while the average B2B response takes 42 hours and 23% never respond at all. Being fast is rare enough to be a differentiator by itself.

Prep checklist (10 minutes, not 60)

  • Open the Opportunity. Read the Campaign Intelligence record — what the machine already knows, which fields are confirmed, what's flagged needs review, and the open questions it raised. Those open questions are pre-written discovery questions; bring them.
  • Read the request itself. Zoom or phone? What did they write in the message field? Which Campaign Brief did they come from — and which sections did that brief lead with?
  • Research the buyer for 5 minutes: agency or brand-direct? Recent campaigns (watch one spot — note what the music does). Holding company (procurement style follows). Who else at the shop might touch music (a producer? business affairs?).
  • Pick ONE teach moment (p. 6) you can deliver credibly for this buyer.
  • Set your anchor range for their likely tier before the call — you will discuss price on this call (p. 6), and you should never improvise a number.
  • Confirm the recorder is armed (Zoom calls). If phone: you'll dictate your read into the debrief lane after. Either way — plan to take zero notes live.

Set the frame in the first 90 seconds

Open with warmth, then an upfront contract — a mutually agreed agenda and outcome. It sets you as a peer, not a vendor auditioning:

"Thanks for grabbing time. Here's what I'd suggest we do with our 25 minutes: I want to understand the campaign and how you're handling music today; I'll share how we work and what it costs; and if it looks like a fit, we'll agree on a concrete next step — and if it doesn't, I'll say so and point you somewhere useful. Sound fair?"

Notes on openers: proactively stating the reason for the call correlates with ~2× success in Gong's data; never open with "did I catch you at a bad time?" — it measurably hurts. On a warm call, "how've you been?" is the single strongest opener they measured (6.6×) — use it when there's been prior contact.

Disqualify with grace

If discovery reveals no real music need, no path to budget, or a locked incumbent — say so: "From what you've described, I don't think we're the right spend for this one. Here's what I'd do instead…" An honest disqualification builds the reputation that fills the shortlist later. Log it; the machine remembers why, and the next campaign is smarter.

Part two · The call

The question arc

The mechanics, from ~519,000 analyzed discovery calls (Gong): top performers ask 11–14 questions, spread naturally through the conversation — not front-loaded like an intake form. They talk about 43% of the time and listen 57% (win rates fall sharply past 65% talk time). Let them monologue; long buyer answers are a buying signal, not dead air.

The arc below is Rackham's validated sequence (situation → problem → implication → payoff) tuned to our domain. Every answer feeds a Campaign Intelligence field — the transcript captures it, so stay in the conversation.

1 · Situation — the campaign (3–4 questions, keep this tight)
Facts you need; don't linger — supervisors live this all day and over-asking here reads junior.
  • "Tell me about the campaign — what's it for, and where will it run?" (media, territory, term — the license scope)
  • "Where are you in production — script, shooting, or already in the edit?"
  • "When does it ship — and when would music need to be locked?"
  • "Who else weighs in on music — creative, business affairs, the client?"
2 · Problem — how they source music today (3–4 questions)
Surface the friction they've normalized. Ask about the last time, not hypotheticals.
  • "How do you usually handle music — library, licensing, a music house you trust?"
  • "Walk me through the last search: how long did it take, and how many revision rounds?"
  • "Ever had a track you loved die in clearance — or a license that couldn't cover the media you needed?"
  • "Who ends up handling the rights paperwork — and how do they feel about it?"
3 · Implication — the cost of those problems (2–3 questions; the money questions)
Rackham's strongest finding: these questions separate top performers in considered purchases. Ask, then be quiet.
  • "If a track fails clearance two weeks before air, what does that do to the schedule — and to your week?"
  • "If the campaign really works and the client wants to extend it to new markets or a second year — what happens to your music license then?"
  • "What's it worth to never have a music-rights question land on this campaign at all?"
4 · Payoff — let them say the value (1–2 questions)
A need they articulate themselves outperforms any pitch of the same point.
  • "If the music showed up composed to the cut, with stems and cutdowns, and one signature covered every right forever — what would that change about how you run this?"
  • "Would it help if the rights documentation were shaped for your procurement team from day one?"

Part two · The call

Teach, qualify, and talk price

Pick one teach moment (never more than one)

If the buyer is…Teach this
Using platform/stock music in paid socialThe Marriott/DSW/Gymshark suits — platform libraries cover personal use; brands are being sued at $150k statutory per work. Most buyers genuinely don't know this.
Curious about AI musicThe July 2026 Sony/Suno ruling is still open; Warner and Universal settled; ownership of AI output is unsettled. 62% of consumers say they disengage from AI music; half of surveyed supervisors won't touch it.
Sync-licensing a known songThe real all-in math: sync + master often doubles the quote, scoped to one term/territory — a custom buyout can cost less than the renewal alone.
A music-house loyalistHow the spec-demo model works against them on speed (composers paid $200–$500 to gamble; ~3-week turnarounds) vs. a committed engagement.

Qualification — what must be true to advance

Confirm these five before proposing anything (they map 1:1 to Campaign Intelligence fields):

1 · Real music need with a date 2 · Budget band acknowledged 3 · Decision path named (who approves, incl. client) 4 · Paper process understood (MSA? PO? terms?) 5 · Competing alternative identified

And begin multi-threading now: won deals typically have 3+ engaged buyer-side stakeholders; lost ones often have one (Gong). On the call: "For the proposal to be useful, it should answer business affairs' questions too — who should I include, or what will they ask?"

Talk price on this call — in the back third

The data is unambiguous: deals where price is discussed on the first call win ~10% more — but top reps raise it about three-quarters through (the 38–46-minute window of an hour), after the implication questions have established what's at stake. Rules:

  • Anchor first and precisely. First numbers set ranges (meta-analytic finding), and precise numbers anchor harder than round ones: say "engagements like this typically land between $18,500 and $27,500," not "about twenty grand."
  • Three options, always (in the proposal): a premium tier that anchors, the recommended middle, a lean entry. Buyers gravitate to the middle — and the top tier makes it feel reasonable.
  • One bundled price, itemized value. List everything included (composition, revisions, stems, cutdowns, rights docs, certification) under a single number. Never surcharge-itemize.
  • Never answer "what's your best price?" with a number. Explore it: "Happy to talk numbers — help me understand what you're comparing us against, so I quote the right scope."
Remember Hinge's finding

Cost decides ~8% of professional-services purchases; sellers assume it's ~54%. The buyer across the table is almost certainly less price-sensitive than your instinct says. Hold the anchor.

The objection table

The pattern for every objection (LAER + labeling): pause — Gong's data shows top reps slow down after objections while weak reps interrupt and counter. Then: Listen fully → Acknowledge with a label ("It sounds like budget's the concern…") → Explore with one clarifying question → Respond. For predictable pushback, run an accusation audit — name it before they do: "You're probably thinking this costs multiples of a library subscription — let me show you what the difference actually buys."

Part two · The call

Land the next step, then follow through

The last five minutes decide the deal

Successful reps spend measurably more time on next steps (~13% more; ~4 extra minutes) — and booking a specific date and time beats an open-ended "I'll follow up" (37% vs 32% meeting-book rate in Gong's CTA data). Never end on "I'll send some stuff over."

"Here's what I'd suggest: I'll send a one-page campaign brief today with two creative directions and the three engagement options we discussed. Can we take 15 minutes Thursday at 2 to walk through it — and should business affairs join so we can clear their questions in the same pass?"

First-deal wedges (when they like you but won't commit)

  • Paid discovery / scoped first brief: a small paid engagement (a cutdown package, a 30-second re-score) proves delivery before the big commitment — the standard first-deal wedge in agency services.
  • Risk reversal: the classic agency-sales example is guaranteeing the outcome or refunding the fee. Ours is structural: clearance certification IS risk reversal — every right, one signature, documented. Say it that way: "if a rights question ever lands on this music, that's our problem, in writing."
  • Start procurement in parallel: at first serious interest, offer your vendor-onboarding pack (W-9, insurance, banking, MSA redlines). It signals professionalism and removes the 4-week paperwork lag from the critical path. Flag sequential-liability clauses; negotiate a deposit.

After the call — the follow-through routine

1
Same hour: open the Opportunity. The transcript has been extracted into Campaign Intelligence — review the proposed fields, confirm what's right, fix what's wrong, resolve conflicts. Your edits are authoritative; downstream documents recompute from them.
2
Same day: send the updated Campaign Brief (it now reflects everything from the call) plus the recap: what you heard, the three options, the booked next step. Speed compounds the same way it did at lead-response.
3
Log your read in the debrief lane ("What's your read?"): risks you sensed, the politics, who the champion is. Interpretation stays separate from fact — that's the point — and it makes the next campaign smarter.
4
Multi-thread within 48 hours: ask your contact to introduce business affairs or the producer ("so the proposal answers their questions too"). Three engaged stakeholders is the won-deal pattern.
The standard you're held to

Every claim in a Chordential call is true, sourced, and current — the honesty rule applies to sales hardest of all. Say "allegedly" on unresolved suits. Never invent a client story. If you don't know, say "I'll find out" — and then be fast about it.

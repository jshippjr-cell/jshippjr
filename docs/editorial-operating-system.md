# Chordential — Editorial Operating System

*Author: Editorial Director. Version 1, 2026-09-08. This document executes strategy; it
does not set it. Anyone joining Chordential's marketing team should be able to produce
publishable work by following this document alone.*

---

## 0. What this is, and what it inherits

This is the operating system for everything Chordential publishes. It answers, for
every channel and every week: what we publish, why, in what shape, made by whom,
checked against what, and reused how.

It inherits four things it may not change. When this document and one of them
disagree, the inherited document wins and this one is corrected.

| Inherited | Where it lives | What it fixes for us |
|---|---|---|
| **Brand strategy** | `docs/design/art-direction-bible.md`, `docs/architecture/CONSTITUTION.md` §3–4, §7 | Warm, precise, made. Restraint is the flex. Honesty is a hard constraint. Procurement-grade always. |
| **Messaging framework** | `docs/cmo-positioning-brief.md`, `docs/product-spec-clearance-certified.md`, `docs/sonic-signature-sales-playbook.md` | Category: *music opportunity intelligence → the OS for commercial music procurement*. The offer the market pays for: *clearance-certified original music, delivered the way procurement wants it*. The line: *the music's the easy part; the certainty is what you're buying.* Five ICP one-liners. |
| **Content strategy** | `docs/cmo-charter.md` (Content Strategist seat), `docs/product-spec-clearance-certified.md` §Go-to-market | Publish clearance / Content-ID / rights education as inbound. Sell the outcome, not the tech. Case studies only when real and client-approved. |
| **Growth strategy** | `docs/company-strategy.md` (A→B→C, lean, relationship-led), `docs/product-spec-clearance-certified.md` §Success metrics | Relationship-led sales to agencies and production companies; pilots → retainers; no paid volume play; capital discipline. Volume is never a success metric. |

Three inherited rules bind every piece we publish and are repeated throughout because
they are the ones most easily broken by a hurried post:

1. **Honesty.** Never imply real client work that did not happen. Never fabricate a
   credit, a capability, a number, or a profile. Demonstrations use invented brands
   (AURORA, Vance Athletic), never real trademarks. When we cannot do something well,
   we say so.
2. **No AI-generated music.** The music is always human-made. Copy says so plainly.
   AI may assist research and drafting under the workflow in §6; it never *is* the
   craft and it never *is* the byline.
3. **The machine proposes, a human disposes.** Nothing is published without a named
   human approver. Automation may schedule; it may not decide.

## 1. First principle — content is a curriculum, not a feed

We never ask *"what should we post today?"* We ask *"what should our audience
understand better tomorrow than they do today?"*

That question has a finite, nameable answer. The people who buy commercial music (agency
producers, executive producers, creative directors, music supervisors, brand marketing
leads) are missing the same six bodies of knowledge, and those gaps are exactly the
pains the messaging framework names: rights risk, delivery chaos, mispricing, and not
knowing how to ask for music. So the whole publishing engine is organised as **six
learning tracks**. Every franchise, every calendar slot and every repurposed post
advances one of them.

| Track | The audience's question | What "understanding it better" looks like | Inherited pain it answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **T1 Rights & Clearance** | "Can my legal team defend this?" | Knows chain of title, work-for-hire vs. assignment, what a licence must cover (media, territory, term, exclusivity), why in-app libraries die under paid spend, what Content ID does, why AI-only output is not copyrightable, why a public-domain song is not a public-domain recording. | Clearance uncertainty; the cross-channel licence trap |
| **T2 Cost & Pricing** | "What should this cost?" | Knows a creative fee is separate from a licence fee, what moves each, why a stated budget is a check and not a price, what revisions cost, when a retainer beats projects. | Mispricing; "don't know what music should cost" |
| **T3 The Brief** | "How do I ask for music so I get what I need?" | Knows what a complete brief states vs. what a studio has to assume, how references help and hurt, what timing and cut information matter, why scope is priced before it is composed. | Winnable work lost to late or bad briefs |
| **T4 Production & Revision** | "How does the work get made and changed?" | Knows versions and cutdowns, longest-version-first, what a note is vs. a change request, what a round buys, what conforming to a new cut means, why timecode matters. | Delivery chaos; revision spirals |
| **T5 Delivery & Proof** | "What does a handover I never have to chase look like?" | Knows a cue sheet field by field, stems and naming, a deliverables matrix, an approval package, a clearance certificate, and why paperwork is the product's handshake. | Delivery chaos; "is this actually cleared?" |
| **T6 The Business Behind the Score** | "How does this industry actually make money?" | Knows how composers, supervisors, studios and agencies are paid, what PROs and publishing do, why free demos are a bad deal for everyone, how a small studio prices its time honestly. | Buyer trust; respect for the craft economy |

**Rule:** a piece that advances no track is not published, however clever. The
Editorial Director keeps the **Track Ledger** (one row per published piece: date,
franchise, track, the single sentence the audience should now be able to say) and reviews
coverage quarterly. A track that has gone six weeks without a piece is the next
priority, regardless of what is trending.

**Rule:** the ledger sentence is written *before* the piece. It is the learning
objective. Example of the form only: *"After this, a producer can tell a note from a
change request."* If you cannot write the sentence, you do not have a piece.

## 2. Stage 1 — Publishing cadence

A cadence is a promise to the audience. The only failure is breaking it. So cadence is
set at the level we can hold for a year with the team we have, and it steps up only
after it has been held.

### 2.1 The consistency ladder

| Tier | Who is producing | When you may move up |
|---|---|---|
| **Tier 0** | The founder, with AI assistance under §6 and no dedicated marketing hire | Start here. |
| **Tier 1** | Founder plus one part-time or full-time content producer | After **8 consecutive weeks** at Tier 0 with ≥ 90 % of scheduled slots published on the day scheduled. |
| **Tier 2** | A marketing team of two or more with an Editorial Director | After 12 consecutive weeks at Tier 1 at the same standard, and a newsletter list large enough that a reply rate is measurable. |

Stepping *down* a tier is always allowed and is not a failure. Publishing sporadically
at a higher tier is the failure. If a channel cannot be held at its tier cadence, it is
paused entirely and announced as paused, never left to trail off.

Never more days. | More than one post per day. Posting on a day the calendar does not name. |

### 2.3 The week's shape

The three LinkedIn days carry fixed jobs so the audience learns the rhythm:

| Day | Job | Franchise slot |
|---|---|---|
| **Monday** | Correct one belief. Short. | Music Myth Monday |
| **Wednesday** | Explain one thing properly. The teaching centre of the week. Carousel-shaped. | Commercial Music Explained · Anatomy of a Cue Sheet · Inside the Delivery · Revision Room (rotating, see §4) |
| **Friday** | Show the work being thought about. Longer. First person allowed. | Behind the Brief · Campaign Breakdown · Producer's Notebook (rotating, see §4) |

Publish in the morning of the buyer's timezone (Eastern for the current ICP list), in
the same hour every time. The hour is a hypothesis; hold it for eight weeks before
changing it, and change it once.

### 2.4 Cadence rules

- **Bank two weeks.** Nothing enters the calendar for a week unless the previous week is
  fully drafted and approved. At Tier 0 this is the only way a founder holds a cadence.
- **A slot is filled or it is skipped visibly.** If a Wednesday piece is not ready, the
  slot is skipped and the ledger records why. It is never filled with something that
  fails §8.
- **Seasonal moments displace, they do not add.** A Super Bowl piece takes the Friday
  slot; it does not become a fourth day.
- **Podcasts and talks are scheduled from the backlog,** not improvised. A pitch to a
  host names the track, the franchise and the three things the audience will leave
  understanding.

## 3. Stage 2 — Editorial franchises

A franchise is a recurring format with a name, a promise and a home. It exists so the
audience knows what they are getting before they read it, and so a new producer can make
episode 40 without asking anyone what episode 40 is.

Every franchise below carries: purpose, audience, format, frequency, expected lifespan,
tracks advanced, its honesty guardrail, and the condition under which it retires. The
**Tier 0 set** (the four a founder runs alone) is marked ★.

### 3.1 ★ Music Myth Monday

- **Purpose:** correct one widely held, expensive misconception about commercial music
  in under a minute of reading. The audience's confidence rises one notch a week.
- **Audience:** every ICP; entry point for people who have never heard of us.
- **Format:** LinkedIn text post (≤ 150 words) with a single typographic image carrying
  the myth crossed through and the correction. Mirrored to Instagram as a single image
  and to X as one line.
- **Frequency:** weekly, Monday.
- **Lifespan:** 52 episodes, then review. Myths are finite. Keep a **Myth Register**
  (myth · the correction · the source · the track); when fewer than eight unclaimed
  entries remain, the franchise is retired or relaunched as a second season.
- **Tracks:** any; weighted to T1 and T2.
- **Honesty guardrail:** every correction cites a checkable source (a statute, an
  office ruling, a platform's published terms, a rate card). "Everyone knows" is not a
  source. Legal claims are reviewed under §6.5.
- **Retire when:** the register runs dry, or the on-time rate drops below 90 % for a
  month.

### 3.2 ★ Commercial Music Explained

- **Purpose:** the encyclopedia. One concept, explained properly, once, so it can be
  linked to forever. The glossary of the industry, written for the buyer rather than
  the composer.
- **Audience:** producers and brand teams who are new to commissioning music; music
  supervisors who need something to send to a client.
- **Format:** carousel (8–12 frames, §5.3) on LinkedIn and Instagram; the same content
  filed as a glossary entry on the website (400–700 words). One video short when the
  concept benefits from sound.
- **Frequency:** Wednesday, two weeks in three (the third Wednesday belongs to
  Anatomy, Inside the Delivery or Revision Room in rotation).
- **Lifespan:** indefinite. This is the reference library; its value compounds.
- **Tracks:** T1, T2, T4, T5.
- **Honesty guardrail:** a definition is checked against the authority we already keep
  (the pricing page and its engines for anything priced; the delivery documentation for
  anything about handover). Never restate a number that lives elsewhere; link to it.
- **Retire when:** never; refresh entries when the fact changes, with a dated note.

### 3.3 Anatomy of a Cue Sheet

- **Purpose:** dissect one real industry document, field by field, until the audience
  could fill one in. The flagship is the cue sheet; the series continues through the
  documents a procurement-grade delivery contains.
- **Audience:** producers, business-affairs and marketing-ops people, junior
  supervisors.
- **Format:** carousel (one field per frame) plus a long-form video (8–15 min) walking
  the document; article version on the website with the annotated document as an image.
- **Frequency:** monthly, on a Wednesday.
- **Lifespan:** 12–18 months. The document set is finite (cue sheet, clearance
  certificate, music licence, deliverables matrix, stems manifest, approval package,
  composer agreement, service agreement, rollout matrix, work-for-hire clause, a PRO
  registration). When the set is covered, it becomes a standing web reference.
- **Tracks:** T5, T1.
- **Honesty guardrail:** documents shown are Chordential's own templates populated with
  an invented brand, or blank industry forms. Never a real client's document, even
  redacted.
- **Retire when:** the document set is covered; later reruns are refreshes, not new
  episodes.

### 3.4 Inside the Delivery

- **Purpose:** show what "handed over the way procurement wants it" actually means, by
  walking a complete delivery for an invented brand from the client's side of the room.
- **Audience:** executive producers, agency producers, procurement.
- **Format:** long-form video (10–20 min) on YouTube, cut into three shorts; a LinkedIn
  article summary; a newsletter feature.
- **Frequency:** monthly, on a Wednesday; at Tier 0 it alternates with Anatomy so the
  founder makes one long video a month, not two.
- **Lifespan:** 12 months, then it folds into the product's own documentation and the
  website's "how we deliver" page.
- **Tracks:** T5, T4.
- **Honesty guardrail:** the delivery shown is a demo (AURORA or Vance Athletic),
  labelled as a demo on screen. The music in it is human-made and credited to its
  composer. Nothing implies a client.
- **Retire when:** the client-facing surfaces themselves teach this well enough that
  the video is redundant.

### 3.5 ★ Behind the Brief

- **Purpose:** show how a studio reads a music brief: what it states, what it leaves the
  studio to assume, what those assumptions cost, what we would ask before quoting, and
  what the job should cost. The audience learns to write a brief that gets them what
  they need.
- **Audience:** agency producers, creative directors, brand managers.
- **Format:** LinkedIn article (900–1,400 words, §5.1) on Friday, followed by three
  derived posts over the next fortnight; a carousel of "stated vs. assumed".
- **Frequency:** every other Friday.
- **Lifespan:** indefinite; format reviewed yearly.
- **Tracks:** T3, T2.
- **Honesty guardrail:** briefs are invented or anonymised composites and are labelled
  so in the first paragraph. Prices come through the same quoting authority the studio
  uses, presented as a band with what was assumed named beside it. Never a real
  client's brief, never a real brand's numbers.
- **Retire when:** never, provided briefs keep arriving to learn from.

### 3.6 ★ Campaign Breakdown

- **Purpose:** take a public campaign made by anyone and explain how the music works in
  it: structure, sync points, how the cutdowns were handled, what the licence probably
  had to cover, what a producer can learn. Criticism as teaching.
- **Audience:** creative directors, producers, composers, supervisors.
- **Format:** short-form video (60–90 s, §5.5) plus a LinkedIn post; a quarterly
  compilation as a long-form video. Seasonal spikes around Super Bowl and holiday
  campaigns.
- **Frequency:** every other Friday, alternating with Behind the Brief.
- **Lifespan:** indefinite.
- **Tracks:** T4, T3, T1.
- **Honesty guardrail:** the credits of the actual makers are named in the piece. We
  never imply involvement. Clips are used under fair-dealing criticism and kept short;
  where the platform makes that risky, we use stills and describe the music. Anything
  we "probably" infer about a licence is labelled as inference.
- **Retire when:** never; the format is the industry's ongoing seminar.

### 3.7 Producer's Notebook

- **Purpose:** the founder's own voice. One lesson a month from actually running the
  studio: a process that changed, a mistake corrected, a decision and its reasoning.
  This is where "cites experience" (§8) is literally true.
- **Audience:** other studio operators, senior producers, the people who will one day
  buy Phase B.
- **Format:** first-person LinkedIn post (300–500 words) on a Friday; leads the
  newsletter that month.
- **Frequency:** monthly (the third Friday).
- **Lifespan:** as long as the founder writes it. It does not survive delegation; if
  the founder stops, it stops.
- **Tracks:** T6, T4.
- **Honesty guardrail:** the lesson must have happened. Client identities are never
  disclosed; the lesson is about our process, not their behaviour. No drafting by AI
  beyond copy-editing, because the value is the person.
- **Retire when:** the founder stops writing, or it starts being written by someone
  else.

### 3.8 Revision Room

- **Purpose:** how a note becomes a version. Notes vs. change requests, what a round
  buys, conforming to a new cut, why timecode matters, how to give feedback a composer
  can act on.
- **Audience:** producers and creative directors who give feedback on music.
- **Format:** short-form video demonstrating one feedback situation on a demo project,
  plus a carousel.
- **Frequency:** monthly, on a Wednesday (takes the third Wednesday in the rotation
  with Anatomy and Inside the Delivery).
- **Lifespan:** 12 months. The topic set is finite; afterwards it folds into
  Commercial Music Explained.
- **Tracks:** T4.
- **Honesty guardrail:** demo project only; the studio's own review surfaces may be
  shown because they are ours, with demo data labelled as such.
- **Retire when:** the topic set is covered.

### 3.9 ★ The Business Behind the Score

- **Purpose:** the monthly long read. The economics of commercial music, explained
  with the respect a buyer deserves: how composers are paid, what a licence fee really
  buys, why retainers exist, what a fair revision policy costs, how a small studio
  prices its time.
- **Audience:** economic buyers (agency owners, executive producers, brand marketing
  directors) and the craft side that wants its economics understood.
- **Format:** website article (1,500–2,500 words, §5.7), summarised as a LinkedIn
  article, featured in the newsletter, and pitched as the podcast topic that month.
- **Frequency:** monthly (published the first Tuesday, so the newsletter can carry it).
- **Lifespan:** indefinite.
- **Tracks:** T6, T2.
- **Honesty guardrail:** every figure has a public source or is explicitly labelled as
  Chordential's own pricing model, linked to the page that owns it. No survey claims
  without a survey. Industry rates are quoted with their date.
- **Retire when:** never.

### 3.10 The Quarter in Commercial Music

- **Purpose:** the quarterly report. What changed in rights, platforms, AI policy,
  awards and rates this quarter, sourced and dated, plus what a buyer should do about
  it. Becomes the spine of the quarter's talk deck.
- **Audience:** every ICP; the piece most likely to be forwarded internally at an
  agency.
- **Format:** website report (2,500–4,000 words) with a one-page summary; a newsletter
  special; a presentation deck (§5.10).
- **Frequency:** quarterly, in the second week after quarter end.
- **Lifespan:** indefinite.
- **Tracks:** T1, T6.
- **Honesty guardrail:** a curated digest of public developments with sources. It never
  claims proprietary data we do not hold. If Chordential's own data is used (win/loss,
  revision counts by segment), it is labelled as ours and its sample size stated.
- **Retire when:** never.

### 3.11 Franchise portfolio rules

- **Tier 0 runs the four starred franchises weekly**, plus three monthly items a
  founder can carry: one Wednesday special (Anatomy · Inside the Delivery · Revision
  Room, in rotation, so there is one produced video a month), the Producer's Notebook
  on the third Friday, and the quarterly report. Making any of the monthly items weekly
  is a Tier 1 decision.
- **One franchise, one home channel.** Derivatives go elsewhere, but the canonical
  episode has one address people can link to.
- **A franchise has a register.** Myths, concepts, documents, briefs, campaigns,
  lessons, topics. The register is the backlog; the calendar draws from it.
- **Retirement is planned, not silent.** A retired franchise gets a final episode that
  says so and points to what replaced it.
- **A new franchise needs the same nine fields** as those above before it gets a slot.

## 5. Stage 4 — Content templates

Each format has a fixed skeleton. The skeleton is what makes episode 40 producible by
someone who did not make episode 1. Fill the beats; do not rearrange them without an
Editorial Director decision.

Every template ends with the same two beats, so they are stated once here:

- **The one sentence.** The ledger sentence: what the reader can now say that they
  could not before. It appears in the piece, usually near the end, in plain words.
- **The honest close.** No call to buy. At most, one invitation to reply or ask, or a
  link to the reference piece. The only thing we ever ask for is a question.

### 5.1 LinkedIn article (900–1,400 words)

1. **Title:** the understanding, stated as a plain claim (never a question, never a
   number-teaser).
2. **Disclosure line** (when applicable): "This brief is a composite" / "Demo brand."
3. **The situation** (≤ 120 words): the concrete moment a buyer meets this problem.
4. **What people believe / what is missing.**
5. **How it actually works** (the body; sub-headed; one idea per section; a table or a
   list where the content is parallel).
6. **What it costs to get wrong** (a real consequence, sourced or from experience).
7. **What to do instead** (a checklist the reader can use on Monday).
8. The one sentence. The honest close.

### 5.2 LinkedIn post (≤ 220 words; Monday ≤ 150)

1. **First line = the claim**, complete on its own, no "hook" tricks.
2. **One paragraph of why**, in the buyer's language.
3. **One concrete illustration** (a demo brand, a public campaign, a number with its
   source).
4. **The one sentence.**
5. Optional: one link to the reference piece. No hashtag walls (three at most, and only
   ones the audience would search).

### 5.3 Carousel (8–12 frames; LinkedIn document / Instagram)

- **Frame 1:** the concept's name and the claim. Studio Light palette; typographic;
  no stock imagery.
- **Frame 2:** why the buyer meets this (the situation).
- **Frames 3 to N-2:** one idea per frame, ≤ 25 words each, in a fixed order that
  builds (definition → variables → example → mistake → check).
- **Frame N-1:** the checklist or the one sentence.
- **Frame N:** the honest close: where the full entry lives. Never a sales frame.
- The post text above the carousel is a §5.2 post, not a caption.

### 5.4 Instagram post (single image)

1. **Image:** one typographic statement in the brand's editorial voice, or one real
   photograph of the studio at work (never staged "content" shots).
2. **Caption:** the §5.2 structure in ≤ 150 words; the first line stands alone.
3. **Alt text** written for the claim, always.

### 5.5 Short-form video (45–90 s; vertical)

1. **0–3 s: the claim, spoken and on screen.** No logo sting first.
2. **3–15 s: the situation.**
3. **15–60 s: the explanation, with the thing itself on screen** (the document, the
   waveform, the cut, the brief). Show, do not perform.
4. **Last 10 s: the one sentence, then the honest close.**
5. Captions burned in; music, if any, is human-made and credited in the caption; a demo
   label on screen whenever a demo brand appears.

### 5.6 Long-form video (8–20 min)

1. **Cold open (≤ 30 s):** the claim and what the viewer will be able to do afterwards.
2. **Chapter 1:** the situation and the misconception.
3. **Chapters 2–4:** the walk-through, one chapter per idea, with chapter markers and
   the real artefact on screen.
4. **Chapter 5:** the mistakes we see, and what they cost.
5. **Close:** the one sentence; where the article and the glossary entry live.
6. Description carries the chapter list, the sources, and the credits (including the
   composer of any music heard).

### 5.7 Blog / website article (1,500–2,500 words; the reference layer)

1. **Title = the claim.** Subtitle = who this is for.
2. **Summary block** (≤ 80 words): the answer, first. A reader who stops here has
   learned the thing.
3. **Definitions** (only the ones this article needs; link the rest to the glossary).
4. **The body**, sub-headed by idea; tables for variables; worked example with an
   invented brand; every figure with its source or its owning page.
5. **What to do**: the checklist.
6. **Sources and dates**, listed. **Last verified** date at the top.
7. The one sentence. The honest close.

### 5.8 Email newsletter (monthly; ≤ 900 words of ours)

1. **Subject:** the month's one understanding, plainly.
2. **Opening (≤ 120 words):** the Producer's Notebook lesson, first person.
3. **This month, by track:** three to five short entries, each one the claim plus a
   link. Never the whole post pasted in.
4. **One reader question, answered** (with permission; anonymised).
5. **One thing that changed** (a rights, platform or rate development, dated and
   sourced).
6. The honest close: reply with a question. Unsubscribe visible. No promotional
   section unless it is a real, dated, buyer-relevant fact (a new package, a price
   change), and then one line.

### 5.9 Podcast outline (for appearances)

1. **The three understandings** the listener will leave with (agreed with the host in
   advance).
2. **Our story in 60 seconds**, told as what we learned, not what we sell.
3. **Per understanding:** the misconception · the mechanism · one concrete example ·
   the sentence.
4. **Questions we hope to be asked** (sent to the host) and **questions we will not
   answer with a guess** (client names, unpublished numbers).
5. **The honest close:** where the reference pieces live. No offer.

### 5.10 Presentation deck (talks; 20–40 slides)

1. **Slide 1:** the claim. **Slide 2:** who this is for and what they will leave able to
   do.
2. **Act I (5–8 slides):** the situation and the cost of the misconception, with
   sources.
3. **Act II (10–20 slides):** the mechanism, one idea per slide, the real artefacts on
   screen (demo brand), one worked example priced through the studio's own model and
   labelled as such.
4. **Act III (5–8 slides):** what to do, as a checklist; what changed this quarter
   (from the report).
5. **Close:** the one sentence; the reference links; credits. No pricing slide, no
   "work with us" slide.
6. Built in Studio Light; text large enough to be read from the back; no slide with
   more than 40 words.

## 6. Stage 5 — Production workflow

Nine stages. Every piece passes through all nine, at Tier 0 in hours rather than days.
Each stage has an owner, an input, an output, a quality standard, and an exit rule.
The ledger records the date each stage was passed.

Roles (one person may hold several; at Tier 0 the founder holds all but Reviewer, which
outside counsel or a named domain expert holds for legal and pricing claims):

- **Editorial Director** owns the calendar, the ledger, the registers, the standards.
- **Producer** researches, outlines, drafts, builds the assets.
- **Reviewer** checks the piece against §8 and, for legal or pricing claims, against
  the authority.
- **Approver** is the named human who says "publish". Today that is Jon. The machine
  proposes; the approver disposes.
- **Publisher** schedules and posts, and files the piece.

| # | Stage | Owner | Input | Output | Quality standard | Exit rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Idea** | Editorial Director | A register entry (myth, concept, document, brief, campaign, lesson, topic) and a calendar slot | A ledger row: franchise · track · the one sentence · date · owner | The one sentence is written and names something the audience does not yet understand. The track is named. | Editorial Director accepts the row. No sentence, no row. |
| 2 | **Research** | Producer | The ledger row | A research note: the claims the piece will make, each with a source (URL and date, or "from Chordential's process" with the person who will vouch), plus the authority page for any number | Every claim has a source. Anything we "believe" but cannot source is either cut or labelled as our opinion. Numbers that live in the pricing page or the delivery docs are linked, not copied. | No unsourced factual claim remains. |
| 3 | **Outline** | Producer | The research note + the template for the format | The template's beats filled in one line each | Follows the template order. The situation is concrete. The checklist exists. The disclosure line is present when a demo or composite is used. | Editorial Director approves the outline (at Tier 0, the founder re-reads it the next morning). |
| 4 | **Draft** | Producer | The outline | A complete draft in the format's length, plus the assets (carousel frames, video script, images) | Written in the brand's voice: warm, precise, made. Plain words. No hype vocabulary (§8.4). No em-dash tics, no rhetorical questions as hooks. AI assistance permitted for structure and copy-editing under §6.6; the Producer owns every sentence. | The draft meets length and template. The Producer has read it aloud once. |
| 5 | **Review** | Reviewer | The draft + the research note | Review notes against the §8 checklist; legal or pricing claims signed off by the domain reviewer | The checklist is completed in writing. A legal claim (rights, copyright, indemnity, licence scope) is reviewed by counsel or a named expert before it can pass. A priced example is checked against the studio's own quoting authority. | Every checklist item is a yes, or the piece goes back to stage 4. |
| 6 | **Approval** | Approver | The reviewed draft | A dated "publish" in the ledger | The approver has read the final version, not a summary. The disclosure lines are correct. Nothing implies a client. | Written approval by a named human. Scheduling before approval is not publishing; it is staging. |
| 7 | **Publishing** | Publisher | The approved piece | The piece live on its home channel at the calendar hour; the canonical URL recorded in the ledger | Posted at the scheduled hour on the scheduled day. Alt text on every image; captions on every video; credits present. The home channel first, derivatives after. | Live, URL in the ledger, on time (or the miss recorded with a reason). |
| 8 | **Repurposing** | Producer | The published piece | The derivative set per §7, each scheduled into later slots | Each derivative stands alone and passes §8 on its own. No derivative repeats the parent's wording. Derivatives are dated across four to six weeks, never dumped. | The derivative set is in the calendar with dates. |
| 9 | **Archive** | Editorial Director | The piece and its derivatives | The ledger row completed: URLs, track, sentence, sources, assets' location, last-verified date; the register entry marked used | Findable in one place by franchise, track and date. Sources stored, not just linked. The glossary updated if a definition was introduced. | The row is complete and the register updated. |

### 6.1 Timing at each tier

| Stage | Tier 0 | Tier 1 and above |
|---|---|---|
| Idea → Outline | Same session, from the register | Weekly editorial meeting, two weeks ahead |
| Draft | Next morning (never the same session as the outline) | Within 3 working days |
| Review + Approval | The day after the draft; legal claims wait for counsel, however long that is | 2 working days; legal claims wait |
| Publish | The calendar day | The calendar day |
| Repurposing scheduled | Within a week of publishing | Within a week of publishing |

### 6.2 Correction policy

When a published piece is wrong, it is corrected in place with a dated note that says
what changed, and the correction is posted as a reply on every derivative. A silent edit
is a breach of the honesty rule. A retraction is a piece.

### 6.3 Reader questions

Every question received (comment, reply, email, on a call) is logged as a register entry
with the track it belongs to. Questions are the best evidence we have of what the
audience does not yet understand, and they are captured the way the studio captures a
lead: in one place, with a source and a date.

### 6.4 Assets

Carousels, video, images and decks are built in Studio Light (`docs/design/art-direction-bible.md`).
No stock photography. Demo brands only. The composer of any music heard is credited in
the piece. Asset files are named `YYYY-MM-DD_franchise_slug_format` and stored beside
the ledger row.

### 6.5 Legal and pricing review

- Any claim about copyright, licensing, indemnity, work-for-hire, Content ID, AI
  copyrightability, or a platform's terms is a **legal claim** and is reviewed by
  counsel or a named expert before approval. The reviewer's name and date go in the
  ledger.
- Any priced example is produced through the studio's own quoting authority and
  presented as a band with what was assumed named beside it. The piece links to the
  pricing page rather than restating its tables.

### 6.6 AI assistance

AI may propose outlines, suggest structure, check a draft against the template, and
copy-edit. It may not originate a factual claim (every claim comes from the research
note), may not write the Producer's Notebook beyond copy-editing, and may not approve.
Nothing is published under a human name that the human has not read in full. No
AI-generated music, ever, including as a bed under a short.

## 7. Stage 6 — The repurposing engine

The studio delivers the longest version first and cuts down from it. Publishing does the
same. **One idea is made once, at full length, and every other appearance is a cut.**

### 7.1 The cascade

For one monthly long read (The Business Behind the Score, or an Anatomy or Inside the
Delivery episode), the derivative set is fixed:

| Output | Derived how | When |
|---|---|---|
| **1 website article** | The canonical long version (§5.7). Made first. | Week 0 |
| **1 LinkedIn article** | The website article cut to 900–1,400 words: summary block becomes the opening, sources become a single link back. | Week 0 (same day) |
| **5 LinkedIn posts** | Five fixed lenses on the parent, one per post: **(1) the claim**, **(2) the mistake** we see and what it costs, **(3) the number** with its source, **(4) the example** (demo brand or public campaign), **(5) the checklist**. Each is a §5.2 post that stands alone. | Weeks 1–5, one per week, in the Friday or Monday slot the calendar assigns |
| **1 carousel** | The article's definitions and variables, one per frame (§5.3). | Week 1, Wednesday |
| **3 short videos** | The three ideas that benefit from being seen: the artefact, the mechanism, the mistake (§5.5). | Weeks 2, 3, 4 |
| **1 newsletter section** | The claim plus a link, ≤ 80 words, in the month's issue (§5.8). | The month's issue |
| **Podcast talking points** | The §5.9 outline's "three understandings", drawn from the article's sections. Filed to the podcast register. | Pitched within the month |
| **Conference presentation** | Act II of the quarter's deck (§5.10) is assembled from the three monthly articles; one section each. | Quarterly |
| **Glossary entries** | Any definition the article introduced becomes or updates a Commercial Music Explained entry. | Week 1 |

Yield: one idea becomes 13 published items plus the deck section over six weeks, with
the audience never reading the same wording twice.

### 7.2 The smaller cascades

| Parent | Derivatives |
|---|---|
| Behind the Brief (LinkedIn article) | 3 posts (stated-vs-assumed, what it should cost, the questions we'd ask) · 1 carousel (stated vs. assumed) · 1 newsletter section · a brief-register entry for a future "brief patterns" report |
| Campaign Breakdown (short video) | 1 LinkedIn post · 1 X line · a quarterly compilation long-form · a "sync points" glossary example |
| Music Myth Monday | 1 Instagram image · 1 X line · at 12 myths, a carousel "12 things buyers get wrong about …" · at 52, an article |
| Producer's Notebook | The newsletter opening · 1 X line · at 12 lessons, the year's long read |
| Commercial Music Explained (carousel) | The glossary entry · 1 short video when sound helps · 1 X line |
| The Quarter in Commercial Music | The talk deck · a newsletter special · 4 posts (one per section) · the next quarter's Myth Register additions |

### 7.3 Repurposing rules

- **Downward only.** A short never becomes the source of an article; the article is
  made first and the short is cut from it. If a post outperforms, the article it should
  have come from is scheduled, and the post links forward to it when it exists.
- **Every derivative stands alone.** It carries its own claim and its own one sentence
  and passes §8 without the parent.
- **Never the same words twice.** A derivative is a new cut, not a paste.
- **Spread, never dump.** Derivatives are dated across four to six weeks so each channel
  sees one idea in one shape per week.
- **Cross-link back.** Every derivative names where the full piece lives. The reference
  layer on the website is the thing all roads lead to.
- **The ledger tracks the family.** A parent row lists its derivatives; a derivative row
  names its parent. Coverage is measured by families, not posts.

## 8. Stage 7 — Editorial standards

A piece is publishable when every line below is true. The Reviewer answers each one in
writing at stage 5; one "no" sends the piece back.

### 8.1 The standard

1. **It teaches something.** The one sentence exists, and a reader who did not know it
   before can say it after. A piece that only announces, congratulates or reflects is
   not published.
2. **It is accurate.** Every factual claim has a source in the research note, with a
   date. Legal and pricing claims have passed §6.5. Numbers that have an owning page are
   linked to it, never restated.
3. **It is honest.** No implied client. No implied capability we do not have. No real
   trademark in a demonstration. Demo, composite and inference are labelled where they
   occur, not in a footnote. No AI-generated music. AI assistance never appears as a
   human's lived experience.
4. **It avoids hype.** No superlatives about ourselves. No "revolutionary",
   "game-changing", "secret", "hack", "unlock", "10x", "nobody tells you", "you're
   doing it wrong". No countdown, no manufactured urgency. If the fact is remarkable,
   the plain statement of it is enough.
5. **It cites experience where it claims experience.** "We have seen" means we have
   seen, and the person who saw it is named in the ledger. "In our studio" means in our
   studio. Otherwise say "in the industry" and give the source.
6. **It respects the audience's intelligence.** Written for a senior producer. Defines
   a term once, then uses it. No explaining what a brief is to people who write them.
   No cliffhangers, no engagement bait, no "agree?", no "comment YES for the guide".
7. **It is never clickbait.** The title is the claim. Nothing is withheld to make the
   reader click. The first line is true without the rest.
8. **It names the makers.** Any campaign, track or document by someone else is
   credited. Any music heard is credited to its human composer.
9. **It is complete on its own.** A derivative does not need its parent to make sense;
   an article does not need the comments to make its point.
10. **It could belong only to Chordential.** Warm, precise, made. If it could have been
    posted by any B2B tool or any music library, it is not ready.

### 8.2 The pre-publish checklist (copied into the ledger row)

- [ ] Track named; the one sentence written before drafting and present in the piece.
- [ ] Every claim sourced and dated in the research note.
- [ ] Legal claims reviewed (name, date). Priced examples produced through the quoting
      authority and linked to the pricing page.
- [ ] Disclosures present where a demo brand, a composite, or an inference appears.
- [ ] No real client implied; no real trademark in a demonstration; no capability
      claimed that does not exist.
- [ ] No hype vocabulary (§8.1.4); no rhetorical-question hook; title is the claim.
- [ ] Credits present for any third-party work or music.
- [ ] Alt text, captions, chapter markers as the format requires.
- [ ] Follows the template beats in order; within length.
- [ ] Honest close: at most one invitation to ask or one link. No offer.
- [ ] Approver has read the final version and signed the ledger row.

### 8.3 What we do not publish

- Anything about a client, named or recognisable, without their written approval as a
  case study. Until a client-approved case study exists, there are none.
- Reaction pieces to a competitor by name.
- Predictions stated as facts.
- Our own metrics as proof of anything unless the sample size is stated.
- Anything whose only purpose is to be seen posting.

### 8.4 Voice, in one paragraph

Write like the studio's paperwork reads: warm, precise, made. Plain words. Short
sentences that carry a verb. Say "we don't know" when we don't. Say "this is a demo"
when it is. Prefer the specific artefact to the abstract principle. The reader is
senior, busy, and will forward the piece to their legal team; write so that the legal
team nods.

## 9. Measuring the system

Consistent with the growth strategy, volume is never a success metric. Three things are
measured, monthly, in the ledger:

| Measure | What it is | Target |
|---|---|---|
| **Consistency** | Scheduled slots published on the day, as a percentage; the longest unbroken streak per franchise. | ≥ 90 %; step up a tier only on this. |
| **Understanding** | Questions received (comments, replies, emails, on calls) logged to the register; newsletter reply rate; saves and shares on the reference pieces; "I read your piece on …" recorded on discovery calls as a first-touch source. | Rising quarter on quarter. |
| **Business** | Inbound conversations that cite a piece, qualified through the studio's own pipeline; nothing else. | Reported, not targeted, until Tier 1. |

Followers, impressions and reach are recorded for diagnosis and never reported as
results.

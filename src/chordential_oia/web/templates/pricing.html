Multiplied together against a baseline job — broadcast, national, three years, non-exclusive, where every factor is 1.0. The baseline is a normal advertising licence rather than the cheapest possible one, so the common case needs no adjustment and the adjustments read as what they are.

Four factors multiplied compound fast and the market does not, so the product is capped at ×{{ '%.1f'|format(cap) }}. Normalised to a common baseline, the published sync card puts “perpetual, worldwide, all media” at about 2.5× a one-year North American licence; uncapped these tables reach 7.6×. Each factor is individually defensible and their product is not — a buyer refusing to pay is a fact no rate card overrides. The cap sits above the market's top so bespoke work, where a buyout transfers a real asset rather than renting a catalogue track, keeps headroom.

Where the creative fee comes from

Cost, then margin

The estimate costs the work from hours × rate, plus players and room. The creative fee is that cost at a {{ (target_margin * 100)|int }}% target margin, quoted in a band of ±{{ (band_spread * 100)|int }}% because the estimate is uncalibrated and says so.

The floor is separate and lower: {{ (min_margin * 100)|int }}% margin on the high end of the cost band. The target is what we aim for; the floor is the line that turns a negotiation into a walk.

What the budget does

Nothing to the price. A stated budget returns a verdict — below our floor, in band, or above band — and that is all it is allowed to do.

A price that moves to meet a number the client happened to say is not a price, it is an echo. Ask for the music number by name: a production budget or a media spend quoted back at you is a number that walks into a proposal.
